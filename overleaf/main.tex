\abstract{
In attempt to understand the ``neural code'', we have developed conceptual and mathematical tools for interpreting neuronal activity across recording modalities. Neural activity is predictable from its own recent past, and that predictability fades with time. In sensory cortices, all forms of neural activity, from spiking to EEG, can reliably predict future neural activity up to a few hundred milliseconds later. In contrast, activity in association cortex predicts subsequent activity up to several seconds later. This temporal dependency of neural activity is often referred to as ``neural timescales''. There are several mathematical models for quantifying timescales: extracting a decay parameter from the autocorrelation function, fitting functions to power spectra, or directly from the time series with autoregressive models. Here, we show in both simulated and real neural data how common neural signal features -- model mismatch, neural oscillations, finite sampling, and dynamic timescales -- can yield inaccurate or uninterpretable timescale estimates. We show that higher-order autoregressive, AR($p$), models, designed to predict future data from $p$ past time points, handle these issues. The AR($p$) model decomposes into a set of modes or components. In autocorrelation, these components are represented as a sum of damped oscillators or relaxation functions, both with exponentially decaying structure. This method is applied to a variety of neural recordings from different specifies, spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Timescales}\label{sec:timescales}

Many real world processes are correlated at varying scales. For example, when trying to predict tomorrow's high temperature, a reasonable starting point is to use the high temperature from today. This also works to predict the high temperature the day after, and the day after that, and so on. One common approach for capturing these temporal relationships is through the use of an autocorrelation, which quantifies how correlated a signal is with itself at every temporal lag. The autocorrelation function (ACF) describes the resulting curve, which can be visualized as the correlation at each time lag (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}).

In natural processes, the ACF often decays exponentially, with correlation strongest at short time lags and decaying as the lag increases. The rate at which the ACF decays from correlated to uncorrelated is usually described as the ``timescale'' of the process. For purely random, Gaussian distributed, white noise processes, the decay is instant -- dropping from perfectly correlated at lag-zero to uncorrelated at all other lags. Timescales are quantified as the exponential decay rate of the ACF, where numerous real-world processes, ranging from the atomic scale with radioactive decay \cite{semkow_oscillations_2009}, to the astronomical with stellar flares \cite{anfinogentov_decaying_2013}, show this temporal dependence. Regardless of the domain or modality, the mathematical basis of timescales is the same, while interpretations and ground truth mechanisms will vary based on the signal's context.

Returning to the temperature example, temperature fluctuates periodically, from day to night and across seasons. These periodic fluctuations introduce oscillations into the ACF. An oscillation is strongly anticorrelated with itself at every half-cycle lag and strongly correlated at every full-cycle lag. Weather does not generate a perfect sinusoids, so the amplitude and timing of each cycle will vary, and the correlation between cycles weakens as lag increases. The ACF of such a process is a damped oscillator: a cosine at the period of the cycle, multiplied by an exponentially decaying envelope. Two parameters describe this, a period and a decay rate, rather than the single decay rate of a purely aperiodic process. Yule described the sunspot cycle in these terms, as a damped oscillator, and introduced the autoregressive (AR) model to do so \cite{yule_1927}.

In this paper we compare the ACF, PSD, and AR models commonly used to estimate timescales, and show how each is biased when the assumed form is incorrect. This is shown in simulation, where the timescale is known, and in recorded data, where it is not, and recommendations are provided for addressing these biases. Importantly, we show how to interpret complex, high-order AR($p$) models from the perspectively of timescales. AR($p$) has a corresponding form in all three domains and can be decomposed into a set of decaying and oscillatory components.

\subsection{Neural Timescales}\label{sec:neural}

In the context of neuroscience, timescales have been observed across recording modalities, from single unit recordings \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014} and neuronal population local field potentials (LFP) \cite{haider_millisecond_2016} to human electroencephalography (EEG) \cite{meisel_decline_2017}, magnetoencephalography (MEG) \cite{zhigalov_relationship_2015}, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, raut_organization_2020}. Neural timescales characterize the length of time in which an input is integrated and maintained by neuronal populations. That is, if an input causes a change in neural activity that then immediately reverts back to random, its timescale will be very short, or fast: you can't predict future values from the current state. However, if an input causes a lasting, predictable structure in activity, its timescale will be longer, or slower: given its current state, you can predict future values. This is interpreted as that activity having a longer ``memory'' of the input. This interpretation of neural timescales as the maintenance of information, or memory, in a neural time series is what makes timescales particularly attractive from a computational neuroscience perspective. However, this is also what makes accurate measurement of timescales so critical, especially given the rapid emergence of studies that examine the functional role of timescales in cognition \cite{cavanagh_diversity_2020, gao_neuronal_2020} and their alteration in disease states \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, wengler_distinct_2020}.

Related to the potential functional role of neural timescales is the non-random structure of neural timescales across the brain. Rather, timescales are spatially organized in a cortical hierarchy \cite{hasson_hierarchy_2008, kiebel_hierarchy_2008, murray_hierarchy_2014, gao_neuronal_2020, misic_brain_2010, raut_organization_2020}. The lowest levels of the hierarchy are associated with short timescales, on the order of milliseconds, in visual \cite{buracas_efficient_1998} and auditory \cite{yang_millisecond-scale_2008} sensory cortices. At higher levels along the cortical hierarchy, longer timescales, on the order of seconds, are associated with decision making \cite{brody_timing_2003} and working memory \cite{wang_probabilistic_2002} in the prefrontal and parietal cortices. This hierarchical distribution has also been observed across species, suggesting evolutionary preservation \cite{buzsaki_scaling_2013}.

Periodic signals introduce oscillations in the ACF. Neural oscillations are embedded within a non-oscillatory, aperiodic signal, which is thought to reflect asynchronous neuronal drive, capturing the timescale of the underlying neural activity \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. This mixing of aperiodic and periodic features means that the ACF also blends aperiodic timescales with neural oscillations. As we will explore in more detail, this mixing of periodic and aperiodic processes results in biased timescale estimates when unaccounted for. High-order AR($p$) can simultaneously decompose multiple timescales and oscillatory processes. The use of AR($p$) decomposition was first applied to EEG to describe delta, theta, alpha, and beta rhythms \cite{Sato1977}.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

Timescales are estimated as a parameter of a model, and is usually a form of the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model: $T_{\text{tomorrow}} = \varphi T_{\text{today}} + \epsilon$. Assuming tomorrow's temperature will be very close to today's, the coefficient, $\varphi$, will approach one. As $\varphi$ approaches zero, today's temperature is increasingly less indicative of tomorrow's temperature and trends towards a Gaussian distributed random white noise process dominated by $\epsilon$.

The AR(1) model may be applied to varying temporal scales. If $T_0$ is today's temperature and $T_k$ is the temperature in $k$-days, we can update the temperature model to: $T_k = \varphi^k T_0 + \epsilon$. In these lagged cases, the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$, depends on the discrete time step, $k$. This is equivalent to the value of the autocorrelation function (ACF) at the corresponding lag step, given the sampling rate, $f_s$. The ACF, $A(k)$, of an AR(1) process is described by an exponentially decaying function and may be alternatively expressed in terms of the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:acf}). The autocorrelation at lag one is equal to $\varphi$. The AR(1) model can simulate a signal, $x$, given a white noise input (eq. \ref{eq:ar}). The ACF and PSD are connected through the Fourier transform, described by the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, so the AR(1) model also has a form in the power spectral density (PSD) (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}).

Timescales, $\tau$, may be expressed as the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:tau}), and are bounded by the scale of the observation, defined by the sampling rate, $f_s$, and the length of the signal, $n$. Sampling rate lower bounds observable timescales, as very short timescales, relative to $f_s$, will be undetectable. For example, if a timescale related to temperature fluctuations is on the order of days, but temperature is sampled once a week, the daily timescale will be impossible to detect. Inversely, the length of the signal upper bounds detectable timescales. If a timescale is on the order of years, but only one week of data is collected, the yearly timescale will be undetectable.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \varphi\, x_{t-1} + \epsilon \label{eq:ar} \\
A(k) &= e^{\frac{-k}{\tau f_s}} = \varphi^k \label{eq:acf} \\
\tau &= \frac{-1}{f_s \log{\varphi}} \label{eq:tau} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \varphi\, e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:psd1}
\end{align}

Neural processes often contain complex structure, beyond what an AR(1) model can sufficiently describe. A solution is increasing complexity through AR($p$) models that use the last $p$ values to predict the next, with one coefficient per lag (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). The ACF and PSD also have corresponding AR($p$) forms. Every past value the model uses adds a term to the ACF, so that the correlation at each lag is built from the correlations at the $p$ lags before it (eq. \ref{eq:arp_acf}), and adds a term to the PSD (eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd}). This lets both take shapes an AR(1) cannot, such as an ACF that dips below zero before returning, or a spectrum with both periodic and aperiodic components.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \sum_{k=1}^p \varphi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon \label{eq:arp} \\
A(k) &= \sum_{m=1}^p \varphi_m A(k-m), \qquad k > 0 \label{eq:arp_acf} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \sum\limits_{k=1}^p \varphi_{k} e\strut^{-j 2 \pi f k / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:arp_psd}
\end{align}

AR($p$) models lose the single parameter interpretion of the process: the timescales. With more than one lag, we must interpret a set of coefficients. Each one measures what its own lag adds beyond the others, so $\varphi_1$ is no longer a correlation and the ACF is no longer a single exponential. However, we will show that AR($p$) model can be factored into a set of processes that correspond to timescales and oscillators.

\subsection{Bias}\label{sec:bias}

Simulations allow the study of timescale estimation bias when analytical solutions do not exist. Timescale estimation is evaluated when the AR(1) assumption fails. Specifically, estimation bias is evaluated under: \textbf{1)} model mismatch, when a process is better described by a higher-order AR($p$) model \cite{broersen_finite_2000}, \textbf{2)} the presence of neural oscillations \cite{berger_uber_1929, buzsaki_neuronal_2004}, \textbf{3)} finite duration or decreasing the number of samples in the signal \cite{broersen_finite_2000, schwarz_estimating_1978, zeraati_flexible_2022}, and \textbf{4)} dynamic, non-stationary timescales associated with behavioral or task states, or neural regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}. The present of oscillations is one type of model mismatch. In general, model mismatch is handled by increasing the complexity of the model to higher-order AR($p$). Finite bias is a data collection problem must be addressed at the level of experimental design.

\subsection{Model Mismatch}

Model mismatch occurs when the AR(1) model is the incorrect model, after other biases have been carefully handled (\figpanel{fig:bias}{a}). This is a generalized extension of oscillatory bias, when there is temporal structure that does not neatly fall into the AR(1) form. In these cases, the AR(1) model is the incorrect model after oscillations have been accounted for, the signal is confirmed to be stationary, and the signal is sufficiently long, yet is not well described by an AR(1) model. When this occurs, the use of higher order AR($p$) offers a solution (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). Increasing the AR order flexibly models PSD and ACF, allowing for forms that deviate from the expected.

The denominator of eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} is a polynomial: $\varphi_1 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^1 + \varphi_2 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^2 + \dots + \varphi_p (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^p$. This allows application of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, suggesting the AR($p$) model can fit any spectral form, i.e. universal approximation, given a sufficiently large order, $p$. The AR($p$) model may overfit other sources of variance, such as finite bias, thus it is desirable to keep $p$ as small as possible. Bayesian information criteria (BIC) may be used to evaluate a set of models with varying $p$ to determine an optimal, minimized AR order \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978, vidaurre_survey_2013}.

Raising the order also has an interpretive cost and has limited the use of AR($p$) models in this literature. A timescale is a single number, and an AR($p$) fit returns a vector of $p$ coefficients. We show in the results section, that AR($p$) model decomposes into an interpretable set of decaying and damped oscillatory modes or components (\figpanel{fig:modes}{}), each with timescale.

\subsection{Oscillations}

In the context of neural data, timescales are typically estimated from the autocorrelation of LFPs or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. However both LFPs and single-unit spiking often exhibit rhythmic fluctuations -- oscillations. Oscillatory activity manifests as a damped cosine in the ACF \cite{brunel_fast_1999} or as a narrow peak in the PSD \cite{he_scale-free_2014, buzsaki_scaling_2013}. An AR(1) model lacks the capacity to represent oscillations (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}).

However, higher order models capture oscillations. An AR(2) process is a damped oscillator in the ACF \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970}: a cosine with an exponentially decaying envelope and a peak of finite width in the PSD. Higher-order AR($p$) decomposes the ACF into a sum of damped oscillators and exponentially decaying components, describing the oscillators and the aperiodic decay together. An early report of this phenomena was in EEG and ECoG, described as a "superposition of damped oscillatory modes" \cite{Franaszczuk1985}.

An indefinitely sustained sinusoid requires perfect amplitude and phase coherence across arbitrarily long intervals. However, neural rhythms wax and wane, drift in frequency \cite{Myrov2024}, appear as brief bursts \cite{feingold_bursts_2015, Jones2016}, and vary in waveform shape \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}. This variability gives rise to damped ACF oscillators. Each oscillatory rhythm is described by a frequency and a timescale, the decay of its envelope. Damped oscillatory modes have been ubiquitously observed across species, recording modalities, and brain regions \cite{Sato1977, Neuenschwander1993, CasteloBranco1998, brunel_fast_1999, Brunel2008, Royer2010, Wallace2011, Kulkarni2020, Lima2011, Lombardi2023} (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{}).

\subsubsection{Finite Sampling}

Stochastic processes suffer from statistical bias related to finite duration recorded signals (\figpanel{fig:bias}{c}). Decreasing the duration of a time series increases timescale estimation bias, limits the maximum detectable timescale, and decreases confidence. Moving towards unbiased timescale estimation approaches is necessary for studying the windows temporal dynamics of task designs (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Dynamic timescales have been observed in working memory \cite{wasmuht_intrinsic_2018, gao_neuronal_2020}, decision making \cite{ganupuru_flexibility_2019} and auditory processing \cite{chien_constructing_2020} tasks.

In the context of event-related task designs or neuronal spiking activity, bias is often mitigated by averaging the ACFs of trials or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In these approaches, when there are multiple neurons, channels, or repeat trials, it is recommended to take the median or mean PSD or ACF across recordings, neurons, and/or time periods, prior to model fitting. Averaging across neuronal populations, regions, and time has been shown to reduce estimation bias attributed to short duration recordings \cite{spitzner_mr_2021}. However, averaging limits the ability to examine between-neuron, region, or trial timescale variability. This suggests that across regions or neurons, signals should be stationary and from similar distributions.

\subsubsection{Non-stationarity}

A signal is stationary when its statistics do not change over time, and non-stationary otherwise. In the terms used here, a dynamic signal is one whose AR($p$) parameters, and therefore its timescale, are not constant across the recording. This occurs when a signal spans varying cognitive or neural states, giving rise to shifts in neural signal statistics and AR parameters. In these cases, a single model combines distinct processes and reports a timescale that belongs to none of them (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Timescales should therefore be estimated from signals that are homogeneous with respect to anatomical or functional boundaries, and to behavioral or task states. This can be done by windowing the recording into periods in which the state is stable, which requires choosing a window size and knowing which windows to combine, or by designing the experiment so that the state is held constant throughout. Windowing carries a cost, since shorter segments increase finite sampling bias and each estimate is less confident, leaving a trade-off between minimizing violations of stationarity and maximizing window size.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We first tested the estimators on simulated processes with a known timescale: an AR(1) process, an AR(2) oscillator, an AR(7) process (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{}). The AR(1) process was also tested under finite sampling with four second simulations. All other simulations were 60 seconds long, minimizing finite bias.

Each process was constructed with a 200 ms timescale so that estimates could be compared on the same axis. We AR($p$) models fit to the ACF and PSD, with order selected by BIC. \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} shows the two AR($p$) fits to the ACF and PSD, along with a fourth column in which the AR(1) process was recorded for only twenty timescales. Results for the AR(1) estimators, time-domain estimators, and a scale-free process are reported in the Supplement.

When the process was AR(1), both estimators recovered the 200 ms timescale within 5\% and less than 1\% of the log spectrum unexplained. For the AR($p$) model, BIC correctly selected $p = 1$.

When the process was not AR(1), the AR(1) estimators disagreed by up to a factor of 112. These disagreements were stable: repeated simulations returned the same estimates to within a few percent. These results show that AR($p$) models with appropriate model-order selection are required when the process is not AR(1), and that sufficient sampling is required for unbiased estimation.

\subsubsection{Model Mismatch}\label{sec:modes}

Increasing the model order corrects model mismatch, but the AR($p$) coefficients are difficult to interpret. Instead, we decompose the model into \emph{modes} (\figpanel{fig:modes}{a}). Each mode is a decay with a time constant $\tau$, and each oscillatory mode also has a frequency $f$. The modes are defined by the poles $\lambda_k$ of the model, the roots of its characteristic polynomial. A real pole is a relaxation mode, whereas a complex-conjugate pair is an oscillatory mode, with
\begin{align}
\tau_k = \frac{-1}{/f_s \log|\lambda_k|}, \qquad f_k = \frac{f_s |\arg \lambda_k|}{2\pi}. \label{eq:mode}
\end{align}
For a real pole, eq. \ref{eq:mode} reduces to eq. \ref{eq:tau}, so an AR(1) model is an AR($p$) model with a single real pole. An oscillatory mode contributes a damped cosine to the ACF, $e^{-t/\tau_k}\cos(2\pi f_k t)$, where $f_k$ is the frequency and $\tau_k$ is the decay of the envelope. The ACF decomposes into a sum of modes, with one exponential decay or damped cosine per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{c}). In the PSD, the mode factors multiply (\figpanel{fig:modes}{b}). Thus, an AR($p$) model describes oscillatory modes as timescale-frequency pairs, $(\tau, f)$. Each mode is also assigned an importance, defined as the amount by which the fitted spectrum degrades when that mode is removed. The AR(7) process in \figpanel{fig:modes}{} has five modes: relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms, and oscillations at 10 Hz and 40 Hz. The four most important modes reproduce its spectrum with $R^2 = 0.997$, while the 2 ms relaxation is negligible.

The modes were recovered accurately from simulated data (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}). Across 200 simulations of 60 seconds, the AR($p$) spectral fit recovered all five modes of the AR(7) process, with median errors in $\tau$ between 1.9\% and 8.0\% and both oscillation frequencies recovered to within 0.6\%. In this benchmark, the three AR($p$) estimators returned 183 to 195 ms for the 200 ms relaxation. These results show that the autoregressive modes, rather than the coefficients, provide interpretable parameters for an AR($p$) model.

\subsubsection{Oscillations}\label{sec:osc}

The AR(2) oscillator in \figpanel{fig:bias}{b} simulates a stochastic oscillation with phase and amplitude drift \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970, feingold_bursts_2015}. Increasing the model from AR(1) to AR(2) corrected the bias in both domains (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, second column). BIC correctly identified the order as $p=2$. The AR(2) fits recovered the 200 ms envelope decay to within 1\% and the 10 Hz frequency to within 0.01 Hz. In contrast, the AR(1) fits returned 10, 139 and 251 ms, depending on the domain. We repeated this comparison across envelope decays from 20 to 150 ms and frequencies from 5 to 40 Hz. The AR($p$) spectral fit remained within 3\% of the envelope decay and the AR($p$) ACF fit within 9\%, whereas the AR(1) estimates were off by up to a factor of 133. An oscillation generated by the process is therefore best handled by modeling it, which also returns its frequency and envelope decay.

\subsubsection{Finite Samples}\label{sec:finite}

\end{figure}

Short recordings bias timescale estimates and reduce their precision, and both effects depend on the ratio of recording duration $T$ to timescale $\tau$ (\figpanel{fig:finite}{}). Propagating the bias and variance of the AR(1) coefficient \cite{mann_statistical_1943, marriott_bias_1954} through eq. \ref{eq:tau} gives a relative bias of approximately $-2\tau/T$ and a relative standard error of approximately $\sqrt{2\tau/T}$. A bias below 10\% therefore requires approximately $20\tau$ of data, while a standard error below 10\% requires approximately $200\tau$, independent of sampling rate. For a 100 ms timescale, these correspond to 2 and 20 seconds of stationary data. Precision improves with the square root of duration, so halving the standard error requires four times as much data. The sampling distribution of $\hat\tau$ is right-skewed because eq. \ref{eq:tau} is convex in $\varphi$: short recordings underestimate the timescale more often, but overestimates tend to be larger (\figpanel{fig:finite}{c}). The AR($p$) estimators show the same effect. For four seconds of the 200 ms AR(1) process, the central 80\% of AR($p$) estimates spanned a factor of five (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, last column).

Finite sampling also constrains the analysis of non-stationary signals. A timescale that changes with task or state requires windows short enough to remain stationary, but short windows are biased and imprecise. Averaging across trials can reduce this cost. Where possible, experiments should be designed so that each state lasts tens to hundreds of timescales, allowing a single window to be both approximately stationary and precise.

\subsection{Empirical}\label{sec:realdata}

We applied the same pipeline to eight publicly available recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity from humans, macaque, mouse and rat (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{}). Timescales spanned three orders of magnitude across modalities, consistent with prior reports of a cortical hierarchy of timescales. Field-potential timescales ranged from 10 to 48 ms using the AR(1) spectral fit, binned spike counts gave 198 ms, and precuneus BOLD gave 2.9 s, with values from 1.0 to 9.3 s across participants.

No recording was well described by an AR(1) model. Every field-potential recording contained a narrowband peak in the PSD, and the ACF oscillated rather than decaying monotonically in six of the eight recordings (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,c}). The AR(1) model left a median of 13\% of the log spectrum unexplained, ranging from 5\% to 52\% across recordings. The AR($p$) model reduced this to a median of 1.5\%, ranging from 0.2\% to 38\%. Because the AR(1) assumption was violated, the estimators disagreed. Across the AR(1) and AR($p$) fits to the ACF and PSD, the ratio of the largest to smallest timescale ranged from 1.3 to 11 across recordings, with a median of 5.4. Including the time-domain estimators, this ratio ranged from 1.6 to 115, with a median of 11. The largest disagreements occurred in recordings with the strongest oscillations, where the AR(1) estimators returned a fast decay and the AR($p$) estimators returned a slower relaxation.

The modes of the AR($p$) fits summarize the temporal structure of each recording (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b,d,e}). After removing modes whose timescales could not be resolved within the analysis band, each trace contained between one and eleven modes, and the figure shows the three most important. Resting-state fMRI was described by a single 3.4 s relaxation mode. Spike counts were described by a 420 ms relaxation and, in half of the units, a slower oscillatory mode near 1.5 Hz. Every field-potential recording required seven or more modes to reproduce its spectrum. For example, posterior EEG contained a 61 ms mode at 10 Hz, a 33 ms mode at 22 Hz, and a 70 ms relaxation; rat hippocampus contained an 82 ms mode at 9 Hz, a 46 ms mode at 21 Hz, and a 71 ms relaxation; sensorimotor MEG contained a 26 ms mode at 15 Hz, a 39 ms relaxation, and a 19 ms mode at 27 Hz; and macaque ECoG contained a 34 ms mode at 15 Hz and a 126 ms relaxation. The modes were consistent across traces within each recording. The 10th to 90th percentile range of the dominant mode's timescale across channels and participants spanned a factor of 1.8 to 8, with a median of 3.3 (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{e}). By comparison, different estimators disagreed by as much as 115-fold on the same traces. These results show that the AR($p$) modes identify the dominant relaxation timescale of each recording while also reporting its oscillatory structure.

Finally, we divided each recording into windows to evaluate non-stationarity (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). In every recording, the spread of estimates across windows exceeded that predicted by finite sampling, by a median factor of approximately 13. This spread includes variation between channels and participants as well as changes over time. A timescale estimated from a long recording therefore averages over a quantity that varies within the recording.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We compared six timescale estimators on simulated and recorded neural data and showed that higher-order AR($p$) models can be interpreted as a set of modes: relaxation timescales and damped oscillations. AR(1) estimators were biased by model mismatch, oscillations, finite samples, and non-stationarity, and these biases could produce estimates that were reproducible but incorrect. The modes of an AR($p$) model were recovered accurately in simulation and described eight neural recordings across six modalities and four species. They were also consistent across channels and participants where the AR(1) estimators were not.

A timescale is estimated as a parameter of a model. For an exponential fit to the ACF, a Lorentzian fit to the PSD, or a lagged regression on the signal, that model is AR(1), and $\tau$ is its relaxation time. When the AR(1) model is incorrect, $\tau$ can remain precise and reproducible, but it no longer measures the relaxation time of the signal. Our simulations show that these cases cannot be distinguished from the estimate alone, and our recordings show that neural data are often not well described by an AR(1) model. Three checks distinguish them. First, independent estimators agree to within a few percent when the data are AR(1), but disagreed by up to 112-fold in simulation and 115-fold in the recordings when they were not. Second, the fraction of the spectrum or ACF left unexplained by the model is small when the model is appropriate. Third, BIC selects $p = 1$ when the data are AR(1). None of the eight recordings passed all three checks.

The four biases require different solutions. Model mismatch can be corrected by increasing the model order, and the modes of the resulting model are interpretable: the dominant relaxation mode is the timescale approximated by an AR(1) fit, while oscillatory modes are reported alongside it with their own frequencies and decays (\figpanel{fig:modes}{}). Every field-potential recording required more than one mode (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{}). An oscillation generated by the process is represented by one of these modes. In contrast, an oscillation added to the signal, such as line noise or a steady-state response, is not autoregressive at any order and must be excluded. This is possible in the PSD but not in the ACF. Finite samples set a floor of approximately $200\tau$ per estimate, while non-stationarity conflicts with this requirement because a window short enough to remain stationary may be too short to provide a precise estimate. In our recordings, the spread across windows was approximately an order of magnitude larger than predicted by finite sampling. Confidence intervals should therefore be estimated from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula. These biases also commonly occur together. Every recording examined here contained an oscillation, had finite duration, and varied over time, so the biases must be considered jointly.

\subsection{Recommendations}

We suggest the following when reporting a neural timescale. \textit{First}, state the model rather than only the estimator, for example `the relaxation time of an AR(1) fit to the 1--100 Hz spectrum'' rather than `the neural timescale''. \textit{Second}, report the ratio of recording length to estimated timescale, and treat estimates based on less than approximately $200\tau$ of data as precision-limited. \textit{Third}, report the fraction of the spectrum or autocorrelation left unexplained by the fitted model. \textit{Fourth}, report at least one independent estimator. For recordings longer than $200\tau$, disagreement beyond approximately 1.5-fold indicates model mismatch rather than sampling error. \textit{Fifth}, if the data contain a strong narrowband oscillation, prefer spectral-domain estimation and state whether the oscillation is modeled or excluded. \textit{Sixth}, if an AR($p$) model is used, report its modes as $(\tau, f)$ pairs with their importances and the number of modes required to reproduce the fit, rather than reporting the coefficients. \textit{Finally}, when windowing, estimate confidence intervals from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula.

\subsection{Limitations}

One major limitation is that our simulations are stationary, while non-stationarity is addressed only through windowing. Non-stationarity remains the most difficult of the four biases because shortening windows to reduce it increases finite-sampling bias. There is also an imbalance across modalities. Seven of the eight recordings are field potentials or a hemodynamic signal, and only one is a spike train. Binning and Poisson variability add a flat noise floor to spike-count spectra that the AR spectral model cannot represent \cite{zeraati_flexible_2022, meyer_inferring_2019}. We addressed this only by restricting the fit band; a model with an explicit observation-noise term would be more appropriate. In addition, the empirical analysis applied one pipeline to every recording without artifact rejection, burst detection, or oscillation removal. The reported mismatch is therefore an upper bound on the mismatch that would remain after dataset-specific preprocessing. The band-limited autocorrelation used here also differs from the raw autocorrelation used in prior work, so timescales from the two are not directly comparable. In one recording, they differed by a factor of sixty. Finally, the mode decomposition is exact for the fitted model but inherits its assumptions. Whether a mode corresponds to a separable biological process cannot be determined from the model alone. For example, the sum of two independent AR(1) processes is not autoregressive, and the fitted poles of such a sum are shifted away from both original timescales.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Estimators}

Four estimators were compared: AR(1)-PSD, AR($p$)-PSD, AR(1)-ACF, and AR($p$)-ACF. They differ in the domain in which they are fit and in model order, while sharing all other steps. Each estimator uses the same mean-centered signal and Welch PSD \cite{welch_use_1967}, so differences between estimators cannot be attributed to preprocessing. In the autocorrelation domain, the model is $A,\rho_p(k) + B$, where $\rho_p$ is the exact AR($p$) autocorrelation, $A$ accounts for a white-noise floor at lag zero, and $B$ is a constant offset. For $p=1$, this reduces to the exponential-plus-offset model used in prior work \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In the spectral domain, the model is eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} with a free scale and is fit to $\log_{10}$ power, following spectral parameterization methods \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}.

\subsection{Autocorrelation}

Spectral fits are restricted to an analysis band, 1--100 Hz for most electrophysiological recordings, whereas the raw sample autocorrelation contains the entire recording, including drift below this band. To fit both estimators to the same signal, we computed the autocorrelation from the same band-limited PSD using an inverse Fourier transform. Frequency bins excluded as line noise were filled by log-linear interpolation between neighboring bins rather than set to zero, preventing notching from introducing ringing in the lag domain. This correction can be large. In rat hippocampal CA1/CA3, the raw autocorrelation gave an AR(1) timescale of 938 ms, driven by slow drift below the analysis band, compared with 14 ms from the band-limited autocorrelation.

A second correction accounts for the Welch taper. Welch's method averages periodograms of tapered segments, so the implied autocovariance is the true autocovariance multiplied by the autocorrelation of the taper. For a Hann window, this decreases to 0.66 at a lag of one quarter of the segment length. We divided out this taper and discarded lags at which it retained less than one fifth of its energy. Models fit to the PSD were transformed to the lag domain using the same procedure: the model spectrum was evaluated on the same frequency grid, restricted to the same band, and inverse transformed (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{c}). The ACF components in \figpanel{fig:realdata}{d} are the partial fractions of the fitted spectrum, one per pole, transformed in the same way. Their sum exactly reproduces the model's band-limited autocorrelation.

\subsection{Robust Regression}

Robust (soft-L1) regression was used only in the spectral domain and only when the signal contained an oscillation: in simulation, for the AR(2) oscillator and the added sinusoid; and in the recordings, for every dataset. An oscillation occupies a small number of spectral bins, so it can be down-weighted as an outlier in the PSD. In the ACF, an oscillation affects every lag, so down-weighting large residuals removes the fit rather than the oscillation. Robust regression therefore involves a trade-off between the aperiodic timescale and the oscillation. For an AR(1) process with $\tau = 50$ ms plus a broadband AR(2) oscillation at 10 Hz, the robust spectral fit returned an aperiodic timescale of 53 ms across 20 simulations while down-weighting the oscillation. Without robust regression, the fit identified the oscillation at a median of 9.4 Hz and returned an aperiodic timescale of 68 ms. Studies that require both quantities should report them from separate fits.

\subsection{Constraints}

Both the autocorrelation and spectral fits were optimized over reflection coefficients rather than AR coefficients. Every point in the box $|k_i| < 1$ corresponds to a stationary model, all parameters are of order one, and the fit cannot enter regions where mode timescales are undefined. Without this reparameterization, 48\% of spectral fits to the recordings returned non-stationary parameters. Multiple starting points were used because the objective is non-convex when an oscillation is present.

Two additional constraints were applied because the model is used to measure a timescale. First, the model must contain a relaxation mode. A converged AR($p$) fit can place every pole in a conjugate pair, leaving no relaxation timescale to report. Before this constraint was imposed, one third of fits to the recordings did so. We therefore factored one real pole out of the characteristic polynomial, $A(z^{-1}) = (1 - a z^{-1})A_{\mathrm{rest}}(z^{-1})$ with $0 < a < 1$, and parameterized the remainder using reflection coefficients. This constraint costs one order and is applied at every order, so BIC selects order three for an AR(2) oscillator. Across the recordings, it changed the median value of each estimator's objective by $+0.003$ in the lag domain and $-0.000$ in the spectral domain.

Second, the reported aperiodic timescale is the most important non-rhythmic mode, where a non-rhythmic mode is defined by $f\tau < 1$: it decays before completing one cycle. A real pole satisfies this definition automatically. The required real pole is not always the most important non-rhythmic mode. For example, in mouse LFP the spectral fit placed the required real pole at 0.6 ms with negligible importance, while the aperiodic structure was captured by an 88 ms conjugate pair with $f\tau = 0.57$. Importance therefore determines which mode is reported.

\subsection{Model Order}

Model order was selected by BIC \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978} in the domain of the fit: from the conditional Gaussian likelihood in the time domain and from the residual sum of squares in the autocorrelation and spectral domains. The search covered a log-spaced grid of orders up to 20. In the lag domain, neighboring lags are strongly dependent, so BIC used an effective sample size equal to the number of lags divided by the lag at which the autocorrelation first falls below $1/e$. When the selected order reached the top of the grid, this was recorded and reported because it indicates a process that AR models cannot represent at any practical order.

\subsection{Modes}

The AR($p$) recursion in eq. \ref{eq:arp} has characteristic polynomial $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, whose roots $\lambda_k$ are the poles. Stationarity requires $|\lambda_k| < 1$. Dividing by $z^p$ gives
\begin{align}
1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} \varphi_k z^{-k} = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (1 - \lambda_k z^{-1}), \label{eq:factor}
\end{align}
so the spectral denominator in eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} factorizes into first-order terms, and each pole contributes a decay time and, when complex, a frequency (eq. \ref{eq:mode}). Because the coefficients are real, complex poles occur in conjugate pairs. Each pair is combined into a single oscillatory mode, equivalent to a real AR(2) factor. The term mode is taken from linear systems theory, where the modes of a system are the eigenvalues that govern its relaxation. This decomposition is a general result for linear time series \cite{west_time_1997} and has been used to track components of a fitted autoregression through EEG seizures \cite{west_evaluation_1999}.

The factorization applies to the transfer function and does not decompose the signal into independent AR(1) processes. The factors multiply in the spectrum, and an AR($p$) process can be represented as a cascade of first-order filters driven by a single input. The same poles appear additively in the time domain. The impulse response is $\psi_n = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^n$, and the autocovariance decomposes similarly,
\begin{align}
\gamma(k) = \sum_j A_j \lambda_j^{,k}, \label{eq:acfmodes}
\end{align}
so after grouping conjugate terms, the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of exponential decays and damped cosines, one per mode. The product and sum forms are the factored and partial-fraction representations of the same rational function. Each residue $A_j$ depends on every pole, so a mode's weight in the autocorrelation is not a free parameter. This explains why a lag-domain AR($p$) fit is biased by an added sinusoid (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}) and why the process variance cannot be divided into independent per-mode shares. In our implementation, both decompositions reproduce the model to within one part in $10^7$.

\subsection{Mode Importance}

Let $g_k(f)$ denote the log of the $k$th pole factor. The model log spectrum is $\log \sigma^2 - \sum_k g_k(f)$, so the modes are additive in log power. Removing mode $k$ and refitting the overall scale by least squares leaves a residual with root-mean-square in $\log_{10}$ power equal to $\mathrm{sd}_f{g_k}/\ln 10$. We define this quantity as the importance of the mode. An Itakura-Saito variant, equal to the asymptotic Whittle log-likelihood degradation \cite{whittle_estimation_1953, itakura_analysis_1968}, produced the same ranking in every case examined. Importance is measured in the log spectrum and is not a share of variance. For example, the 40 Hz mode in \figpanel{fig:modes}{} ranks second in importance but accounts for only $2 \times 10^{-5}$ of the variance.

The error of a model retaining a subset of modes is a quadratic form in the retained set, so the best subset of each size was evaluated in closed form. Dominant modes are defined as the smallest subset that reproduces the fitted log spectrum with $R^2 \ge 0.99$. This count depends on the cutoff. At $R^2 \ge 0.95$, the field-potential recordings required 6 to 11 modes; at $0.99$, they required 7 to 11; and at $0.90$, they required 1 to 6. We report the strictest cutoff. The three modes shown in \figpanel{fig:realdata}{} are the most important under all three cutoffs.

For the recordings, a mode was reported only if the analysis band could resolve its timescale. A mode with a timescale longer than $1/(2\pi f_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $f_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the lower edge of the fit band, has its knee below the band, so its timescale is extrapolated beyond the data. These near-unit-root poles report timescales longer than the recording and have high importance because removing them changes the lowest frequencies. This criterion removed 7% of fitted spectral modes. An oscillatory mode was counted as detected only if its frequency fell within the fit band.

\subsection{Simulations}

Four processes were used in the benchmark, each with a closed-form PSD and ACF and simulated at 500 Hz for 100 s with 200 repetitions. Each process with a timescale was constructed with the same 200 ms timescale so that estimates could be compared on one axis. \textit{AR(1)} with $\tau = 200$ ms served as the positive control. The \textit{AR(2) oscillator} has poles at $|\lambda| e^{\pm i\theta}$, with $|\lambda| = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}$ and $\theta = 2\pi f_0/f_s$, where $f_0 = 10$ Hz and $\tau = 200$ ms, corresponding to two cycles. The \textit{AR($p$)} process is the AR(7) process shown in \figpanel{fig:modes}{}, with relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms and oscillations at 10 Hz (150 ms envelope) and 40 Hz (60 ms envelope). It is the only process in the benchmark that is autoregressive but higher than first order, allowing order mismatch to be separated from broader model mismatch. Its two slow relaxations have comparable importance and differ by an order of magnitude, while its oscillations persist for more than one cycle, making the 200 ms relaxation the most important non-rhythmic mode. The \textit{Power law} is a long-memory ARFIMA process \cite{granger_introduction_1980, hosking_fractional_1981} simulated by circulant embedding \cite{davies_tests_1987}. It has no characteristic timescale (Supplementary Results).

The finite-sample column in \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} uses the AR(1) process simulated for four seconds, with the Welch window and autocorrelation set to one quarter of the recording duration, as in the windowing analysis. An alternative higher-order process, an AR(5) with a 250 ms relaxation, a 5 Hz mode with $f\tau = 0.5$, and a 50 Hz oscillation, was simulated alongside the AR(7) and can replace it in every figure.

Two parameter sweeps extend the benchmark. The damped-oscillator sweep varied the AR(2) oscillator across envelope decays of 20, 50 and 150 ms and frequencies of 5, 10, 20 and 40 Hz. The added-sinusoid sweep added $A\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)$ with random phase to an AR(1) process with $\tau = 50$ ms, across six frequencies from 4 to 40 Hz and six power ratios from one eighth to four (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). Both sweeps used 20 repetitions per condition. For processes that are not AR(1), the target timescale is defined by applying the same rule as the estimator to the true poles: the relaxation time for AR(1), the envelope decay for the AR(2) oscillator, and the most important non-rhythmic mode for the AR(7). All theoretical spectra and autocorrelations were verified against long simulations.

\subsection{Finite Samples}

For a mean-corrected least-squares AR(1) fit on $n$ samples, the bias is $\mathbb{E}[\hat\varphi] - \varphi \approx -(1+3\varphi)/n$ \cite{marriott_bias_1954, kendall_note_1954}, and the variance is
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[ (\hat{\varphi} - \varphi)^2 ] &= \frac{1 - \varphi^2}{n-1}. \label{eq:var}
\end{align}
Propagating both through eq. \ref{eq:tau} to second order, with $T = n/f_s$ as the recording duration and $\tau f_s \gg 1$, gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] - \tau}{\tau} \approx -\frac{2\tau}{T}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}[\hat\tau]}{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{T}}. \label{eq:finite}
\end{align}
Both quantities depend on the recording only through $T/\tau$, and both were verified with Monte Carlo simulations. Estimation error is therefore reported as the ratio $\hat\tau/\tau$, which has the same distribution for a 10 ms and a 1 s timescale at the same $T/\tau$, whereas absolute and squared errors scale with $\tau$ and $\tau^2$. Confidence intervals were computed by mapping the interval for $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau}, which preserves coverage because the mapping is monotonic. The intervals in \figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b} are the least-squares intervals from eq. \ref{eq:var}, evaluated at each estimate and averaged across simulations. The weighted least-squares/Cauchy construction \cite{gallagher_small_2008} improves these intervals when $\varphi$ is very close to one.

\subsection{Datasets}

Eight publicly available recordings were selected to span species, recording modality, and oscillatory regime. \textit{fMRI}: ABIDE resting-state BOLD \cite{di_martino_autism_2014}, using twenty participants from the UM site, preprocessed with C-PAC without temporal filtering, TR 2 s, and left and right precuneus. The unfiltered pipeline was used because the standard 0.01--0.1 Hz band-pass removes most of the spectrum used to estimate a timescale. A single region was used rather than averaging across regions because regions differ in the property being measured. \textit{MEG}: the FieldTrip cortico-muscular coherence recording \cite{schoffelen_neuronal_2005, oostenveld_fieldtrip_2011}, using the 24 sensorimotor magnetometers with the strongest beta oscillation, recorded during isometric contraction. \textit{EEG}: MPI-LEMON eyes-closed rest \cite{babayan_mind_2019}, using four posterior channels from each of five participants. \textit{ECoG}: primary motor cortex from 23 participants with Parkinson disease before DBS \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}, and 24 channels from macaque visual cortex during a grating task \cite{nagasaka_multidimensional_2011}. \textit{LFP}: 24 channels from mouse Neuropixels recordings in VISam during natural movie viewing \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, and 24 channels from rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 \cite{mizuseki_theta_2009, mizuseki_hc3_2013}. \textit{Spiking}: spike counts from 24 VISp units in the same Neuropixels session \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, binned at 50 ms over the same 300 s natural movie.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Electrophysiological recordings were resampled to 250 Hz and fit over 1--100 Hz, with line-noise fundamentals and harmonics excluded from spectral fits. The MEG recording was fit over 1--95 Hz because of an artifactual peak at 100 Hz. PSDs were computed using Welch's method with 4 s windows and 50% overlap. For fMRI, sampled every 2 s, the window was 128 s and the fit band was 0.01--0.24 Hz. For spike counts, binned at 50 ms, the window was 60 s and the fit band was 0.05--2 Hz rather than the available 0.05--8 Hz. A spike train contributes a flat Poisson floor to the PSD that the AR spectral model cannot represent, and above approximately 1.5 Hz the binned-count spectrum is within a factor of 1.5 of that floor. When fit over the full band, the AR(1) model left 95% of the spike-count log spectrum unexplained; restricting the band improved this tenfold. No other filtering, artifact rejection, or oscillation removal was applied. Halving or doubling the spectral window changed the median AR($p$) spectral timescale by less than 5% in motor ECoG, mouse LFP and rat LFP; by 6--18% in MEG, macaque ECoG, fMRI and spike counts; and by approximately 50% in EEG, which has the shortest traces at 60 s.

\subsection{Windowing}

Each recording was divided into non-overlapping windows of increasing duration, beginning at 0.5 s, and each window was analyzed using the same pipeline. The Welch window and autocorrelation were set to one quarter of the window duration (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Windows were pooled across traces within each recording, so the spread across windows includes variation between channels and participants as well as variation over time. Window duration is expressed in units of the estimated timescale because a 1 s window is long relative to a 4 ms timescale but short relative to a 1 s timescale. The spread of estimates across windows of a given duration was compared with the AR(1) sampling bound from eq. \ref{eq:finite} at that duration.

\subsection{Thresholds}

Three thresholds are used in the text. The $200\tau$ floor follows from eq. \ref{eq:finite}: at this duration, the relative standard error of a single estimate is 10%. The 1.5-fold threshold for disagreement between estimators follows from the same equation. Two independent estimates at $T = 200\tau$ each have a 10% standard error, so their ratio exceeds 1.5 in less than 1% of cases from sampling alone. In the AR(1) simulations at $T = 500\tau$, the AR($p$) fits to the ACF and PSD disagreed by more than 1.5-fold in 4.5% of repetitions, while the AR(1) fits never did. Below $200\tau$, this threshold does not apply: at $T = 20\tau$, sampling alone caused the two AR($p$) fits to disagree by more than 1.5-fold in half of the repetitions. The $f\tau < 1$ criterion for a non-rhythmic mode is a definition: a mode that decays before completing one cycle is treated as a damped relaxation rather than an oscillation.

\subsection{Code Availability}

All analyses use an open-source Python module with 200 unit tests. The tests verify exact reconstruction of the AR($p$) PSD and ACF from the mode decomposition, agreement between closed-form mode importance and brute-force mode deletion, agreement between each theoretical PSD and ACF and long simulations, the relaxation-mode constraint, and agreement between eq. \ref{eq:finite} and Monte Carlo simulations. Each figure is generated by a dedicated script from cached result tables, and the simulations, tables, and figures are tracked as a single reproducible pipeline. Code, figure scripts, result tables, and extracted traces are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}. All recordings are publicly available: ABIDE, MPI-LEMON, the MNE/FieldTrip CMC dataset, Cole et al. 2017, NeuroTycho, the Allen Brain Observatory, and CRCNS hc-3.

\subsubsection*{Benchmark}

For the AR(2) oscillator, the three AR(1) estimators returned 10, 139 and 251 ms for the 200 ms envelope decay, whereas all three AR($p$) estimators recovered it to within 1.1% and recovered the frequency to within 0.01 Hz. Across the damped-oscillator sweep, the AR($p$) spectral fit remained within 3% of the envelope decay and the AR($p$) ACF fit within 9%, whereas the AR(1) estimates were off by factors between 0.016 and 133, depending on both decay and frequency. For the AR(7) process, the three AR($p$) estimators returned 183 to 195 ms for the 200 ms relaxation, whereas the three AR(1) estimators spanned a factor of 112: 179 ms from the ACF fit, 1.57 s from lagged regression, and 20 s, the upper bound of the search range, from the spectral fit. The power-law process produced estimates from 4 to 86 ms, a twenty-fold range, with each estimate stable across repetitions. For this process, the lag-domain estimate was also proportional to recording duration, $\hat\tau \propto T$ across three decades, whereas the same estimator remained constant for the AR(1) process.

The unexplained variance increased with estimation error. The AR(1) spectral fit left 0.7% of the log spectrum unexplained for the AR(1) process, 27% for the AR(2) oscillator, 67% for the AR(7), and 26% for the power law. The AR($p$) fit reduced these values to between 0.03% and 4.0%. BIC selected $p=1$ for AR(1), $p=3$ for the oscillator (its true order plus the required real pole), $p=8$ for the AR(7), and the top of the search range for the power law. When the exact autocorrelation of each process was fit, mismatch was zero at the correct order for all three autoregressive processes, while it decreased without reaching zero for the power law.

The fitting domain also affected the resulting model. Across the three non-AR(1) processes, spectral fits described the autocorrelation nearly as well as autocorrelation fits, leaving 0.2% to 7% unexplained. In contrast, autocorrelation fits described the spectrum poorly, leaving 15% unexplained for the oscillator, 11% for the AR(7), and more than the total spectral variance for the power law. Both domains used the same model, reparameterization, order selection, and analysis band, so this difference is not an optimization artifact. Models estimated in the spectral domain described the signal well in both domains, whereas models estimated in the lag domain did not.

\subsubsection*{Added Sinusoids}

A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is not autoregressive at any order: its PSD contains a line and its ACF contains an undamped cosine (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). As the sinusoid's power increased from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, the AR(1) ACF estimate decreased from the true 50 ms to 3 ms. Increasing the model order did not correct this bias: the AR($p$) ACF estimate ranged from 0.5 to 12 times the true value across the same grid. The weight of each mode in an AR($p$) autocorrelation is determined by the poles, and no set of poles can reproduce the mixing weight of an exponential and an undamped cosine. In the PSD, the sinusoid occupied only a few bins, robust regression excluded them, and both spectral fits remained within 3% of the true value at every frequency and power.

\subsubsection*{Windowing}

In units of the estimated timescale, the median AR($p$) spectral estimate converged to within 20% of its longest-window value once windows exceeded 40 to 2300 timescales, depending on the recording, with a median of approximately 550. In the shortest windows, the median estimate ranged from 13% to 82% of the longest-window value. When the autocorrelation was computed from the raw signal rather than the analysis band, estimates decreased by up to two orders of magnitude in the shortest windows and increased steadily with window duration. This pattern resembles a dependence of neural timescale on window length, but is instead caused by drift below the fit band entering the lag domain. In the longest windows, every estimator exceeded the sampling bound from eq. \ref{eq:finite} in all eight recordings, by a median factor of 11 to 17 and by as much as 44. The band-limited autocorrelation did not remove this excess.

\subsubsection*{Integrated Timescale}

As a model-free reference, we use $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^\infty \rho(t),dt$ \cite{sokal_monte_1997, priestley_spectral_1981}, evaluated using the trapezoid convention so that a sampled exponential returns its continuous-time $\tau$ rather than $\tau + 1/(2f_s)$. Equivalently, $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = S(0)/(4\gamma(0))$. It equals $\tau$ for an AR(1) process, is the variance-weighted mean of component timescales for a sum of independent AR(1) processes, and diverges for a long-memory process. It differs from the envelope decay of an oscillator because the negative lobes of a damped cosine cancel much of the integral. For the AR(2) oscillator in the benchmark, $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = 2.5$ ms while the envelope decay is 200 ms. Thus, for an oscillatory process, the integrated timescale and envelope decay measure different properties of the signal.

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:smethods}

\subsubsection*{Conventions}

The AR($p$) model is written as in eq. \ref{eq:arp}, so its characteristic polynomial is $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, and stationarity requires all roots to lie inside the unit circle. Sign conventions differ across texts, and the two common characteristic polynomials are reciprocals of one another. We use this convention throughout, so a pole inside the unit circle corresponds to a stationary process. The one-sided PSD is
\begin{align}
S(f) = \frac{2\sigma^2/f_s}{\left|1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\varphi_k e^{-i 2\pi f k/f_s}\right|^{2}}, \qquad 0 \le f \le f_s/2,
\end{align}
normalized so that its integral equals the process variance, matching Welch's method. For $p=1$, this is the discrete-time Lorentzian with knee frequency $1/(2\pi\tau)$. The theoretical autocovariance was computed by solving the Yule-Walker equations for the first $p+1$ lags and recursively computing later lags. A fit with a pole at $|\lambda| \ge 1$ can be made stationary by reflecting the pole to $1/\bar\lambda$ and dividing the scale by $|\lambda|^2$, which leaves the model spectrum unchanged.
