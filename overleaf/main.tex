\abstract{
In attempt to understand the ``neural code'', we have developed conceptual and mathematical tools for interpreting neuronal activity across recording modalities. Neural activity is predictable from its own recent past, and that predictability fades with time. In sensory cortices, all forms of neural activity, from spiking to EEG, can reliably predict future neural activity up to a few hundred milliseconds later. In contrast, activity in association cortex predicts subsequent activity up to several seconds later. This temporal dependency of neural activity is often referred to as ``neural timescales''. There are several mathematical models for quantifying timescales: extracting a decay parameter from the autocorrelation function, fitting functions to power spectra, or directly from the time series with autoregressive models. Here, we show in both simulated and real neural data how common neural signal features -- model mismatch, neural oscillations, finite sampling, and dynamic timescales -- can yield inaccurate or uninterpretable timescale estimates. We show that higher-order autoregressive, AR($p$), models, designed to predict future data from $p$ past time points, handle these issues. The AR($p$) model decomposes into a set of modes or components. In autocorrelation, these components are represented as a sum of damped oscillators or relaxation functions, both with exponentially decaying structure. This method is applied to a variety of neural recordings from different specifies, spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Timescales}\label{sec:timescales}

Many real world processes are correlated at varying scales. For example, when trying to predict tomorrow's high temperature, a reasonable starting point is to use the high temperature from today. This also works to predict the high temperature the day after, and the day after that, and so on. One common approach for capturing these temporal relationships is through the use of an autocorrelation, which quantifies how correlated a signal is with itself at every temporal lag. The autocorrelation function (ACF) describes the resulting curve, which can be visualized as the correlation at each time lag (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}).

In natural processes the ACF often decays exponentially, with correlation strongest at short time lags and decaying as the lag increases. The rate at which the ACF decays from correlated to uncorrelated is usually described as the ``timescale'' of the process. For purely random, Gaussian distributed, white noise processes, the decay is instant -- dropping from perfectly correlated at lag-zero to uncorrelated at all other lags. Timescales are quantified as the exponential decay rate of the ACF, where numerous real-world processes, ranging from the atomic scale with radioactive decay \cite{semkow_oscillations_2009}, to the astronomical with stellar flares \cite{anfinogentov_decaying_2013}, show this temporal dependence. Regardless of the domain or modality, the mathematical basis of timescales is the same, while interpretations and ground truth mechanisms will vary based on the signal's context.

Returning to the temperature example, temperature fluctuates periodically, on a day/night and seasonal basis. These periodic fluctuations introduce oscillations into the ACF. An oscillation is strongly anticorrelated with itself at every half-cycle lag and strongly correlated at every full-cycle lag. Weather does not generate a perfect sinusoids, so the amplitude and timing of each cycle will vary, and the correlation between cycles weakens as lag increases. The ACF of such a process is a damped oscillator: a cosine at the period of the cycle, multiplied by an exponentially decaying envelope. Two parameters describe this, a period and a decay rate, rather than the single decay rate of a purely aperiodic process. Yule described the sunspot cycle in these terms, as a damped oscillator, and introduced the autoregressive (AR) model to do so \cite{yule_1927}.

In this paper we compare the ACF, PSD, and AR models commonly used to estimate timescales, and show how each is biased when the assumed form is incorrect. This is shown in simulation, where the timescale is known, and in recorded data, where it is not, and recommendations are provided for addressing these biases. Imporantly, we show how to interpret complex, high-order AR($p$) models from the perspectively of timescales. AR($p$) has a corresponding form in all three domains and can be decomposed into a set of decaying and oscillatory components.

\subsection{Neural Timescales}\label{sec:neural}

In the context of neuroscience, timescales have been observed across recording modalities, from single unit recordings \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014} and neuronal population local field potentials (LFP) \cite{haider_millisecond_2016} to human electroencephalography (EEG) \cite{meisel_decline_2017}, magnetoencephalography (MEG) \cite{zhigalov_relationship_2015}, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, raut_organization_2020}. Neural timescales characterize the length of time in which an input is integrated and maintained by neuronal populations. That is, if an input causes a change in neural activity that then immediately reverts back to random, its timescale will be very short, or fast: you can't predict future values from the current state. However, if an input causes a lasting, predictable structure in activity, its timescale will be longer, or slower: given its current state, you can predict future values. This is interpreted as that activity having a longer ``memory'' of the input. This interpretation of neural timescales as the maintenance of information, or memory, in a neural time series is what makes timescales particularly attractive from a computational neuroscience perspective. However, this is also what makes accurate measurement of timescales so critical, especially given the rapid emergence of studies that examine the functional role of timescales in cognition \cite{cavanagh_diversity_2020, gao_neuronal_2020} and their alteration in disease states \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, wengler_distinct_2020}.

Related to the potential functional role of neural timescales is the non-random structure of neural timescales across the brain. Rather, they are spatially organized in a cortical hierarchy \cite{hasson_hierarchy_2008, kiebel_hierarchy_2008, murray_hierarchy_2014, gao_neuronal_2020, misic_brain_2010, raut_organization_2020}. The lowest levels of the hierarchy are associated with short timescales, on the order of milliseconds, in visual \cite{buracas_efficient_1998} and auditory \cite{yang_millisecond-scale_2008} sensory cortices. At higher levels along the cortical hierarchy, longer timescales, on the order of seconds, are associated with decision making \cite{brody_timing_2003} and working memory \cite{wang_probabilistic_2002} in the prefrontal and parietal cortices. This hierarchical distribution has also been observed across species, suggesting evolutionary preservation \cite{buzsaki_scaling_2013}. The current work focuses on neural timescales, rather than 2D and 3D spatial extensions, e.g. spatial scales. However, the spatial distribution of timescales means that a timescale should be estimated within a local region that is approximately stationary.

Periodic signals introduce oscillations in the ACF. Neural oscillations are embedded within a non-oscillatory, aperiodic signal, which is thought to reflect asynchronous neuronal drive, capturing the timescale of the underlying neural activity \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. This mixing of aperiodic and periodic features means that the ACF also blends aperiodic timescales with neural oscillations. As we will explore in more detail below, this mixing of periodic and aperiodic processes can result in large biases and errors in estimates of the timescale from the ACF.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

Timescales are estimated as a parameter of a model, and is usually a form of the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model: $T_{\text{tomorrow}} = \varphi T_{\text{today}} + \epsilon$. Assuming tomorrow's temperature will be very close to today's, the coefficient, $\varphi$, will approach one. As $\varphi$ approaches zero, today's temperature is increasingly less indicative of tomorrow's temperature and trends towards a Gaussian distributed random white noise process dominated by $\epsilon$.

The AR(1) model may be applied to varying temporal scales. If $T_0$ is today's temperature and $T_k$ is the temperature in $k$-days, we can update the temperature model to: $T_k = \varphi^k T_0 + \epsilon$. In these lagged cases, the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$, depends on the discrete time step, $k$. This is equivalent to the value of the autocorrelation function (ACF) at the corresponding lag step, given the sampling rate, $f_s$. The ACF, $A(k)$, of an AR(1) process is described by an exponentially decaying function and may be alternatively expressed in terms of the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:acf}). The autocorrelation at lag one is equal to $\varphi$. The AR(1) model can simulate a signal, $x$, given a white noise input (eq. \ref{eq:ar}). The ACF and PSD are connected through the Fourier transform, described by the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, so the AR(1) model also has a form in the power spectral density (PSD) (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}).

Timescales, $\tau$, may be expressed as the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:tau}), and are bounded by the scale of the observation, defined by the sampling rate, $f_s$, and the length of the signal, $n$. Sampling rate lower bounds observable timescales, as very short timescales, relative to $f_s$, will be undetectable. For example, if a timescale related to temperature fluctuations is on the order of days, but temperature is sampled once a week, the daily timescale will be impossible to detect. Inversely, the length of the signal upper bounds detectable timescales. If a timescale is on the order of years, but only one week of data is collected, the yearly timescale will be undetectable.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \varphi\, x_{t-1} + \epsilon \label{eq:ar} \\
A(k) &= e^{\frac{-k}{\tau f_s}} = \varphi^k \label{eq:acf} \\
\tau &= \frac{-1}{f_s \log{\varphi}} \label{eq:tau} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \varphi\, e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:psd1}
\end{align}

Neural processes often contain complex structure, beyond what an AR(1) model can sufficiently describe. A solution is increasing complexity through AR($p$) models that use the last $p$ values to predict the next, with one coefficient per lag (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). The ACF and PSD also have corresponding AR($p$) forms. Every past value the model uses adds a term to the ACF, so that the correlation at each lag is built from the correlations at the $p$ lags before it (eq. \ref{eq:arp_acf}), and adds a term to the PSD (eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd}). This lets both take shapes an AR(1) cannot, such as an ACF that dips below zero before returning, or a spectrum with both periodic and aperiodic components.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \sum_{k=1}^p \varphi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon \label{eq:arp} \\
A(k) &= \sum_{m=1}^p \varphi_m A(k-m), \qquad k > 0 \label{eq:arp_acf} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \sum\limits_{k=1}^p \varphi_{k} e\strut^{-j 2 \pi f k / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:arp_psd}
\end{align}

AR($p$) models lose the single parameter interpretion of the process: the timescales. With more than one lag, we must interpret a set of coefficients. Each one measures what its own lag adds beyond the others, so $\varphi_1$ is no longer a correlation and the ACF is no longer a single exponential. However, we will show that AR($p$) model can be factored into a set of processes that correspond to timescales and oscillators.

\section{Bias}\label{sec:bias}

Simulations allow the study of timescale estimation bias when analytical solutions do not exist. Timescale estimation is evaluated when the AR(1) assumption fails. Specifically, estimation bias is evaluated under: \textbf{1)} model mismatch, when a process is better described by a higher-order AR($p$) model \cite{broersen_finite_2000}, \textbf{2)} the presence of neural oscillations \cite{berger_uber_1929, buzsaki_neuronal_2004}, \textbf{3)} finite duration or decreasing the number of samples in the signal \cite{broersen_finite_2000, schwarz_estimating_1978, zeraati_flexible_2022}, and \textbf{4)} dynamic, non-stationary timescales associated with behavioral or task states, or neural regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}.

The present of oscillations is one type of model mismatch. In general, model mismatch is handeled by increasing the complexity of the model to AR($p$). Finite bias is a data collection problem must be addressed at the level of experimental design.

\subssection{Model Mismatch}

Model mismatch occurs when the AR(1) model is the incorrect model, after other biases have been carefully handled (\figpanel{fig:bias}{a}). This is a generalized extension of oscillatory bias, when there is temporal structure that does not neatly fall into the AR(1) form. In these cases, the AR(1) model is the incorrect model after oscillations have been accounted for, the signal is confirmed to be stationary, and the signal is sufficiently long, yet is not well described by an AR(1) model. When this occurs, the use of higher order AR($p$) offers a solution (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). Increasing the AR order flexibly models PSD and ACF, allowing for forms that deviate from the expected.

The denominator of eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} is a polynomial: $\varphi_1 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^1 + \varphi_2 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^2 + \dots + \varphi_p (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^p$. This allows application of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, suggesting the AR($p$) model can fit any spectral form, i.e. universal approximation, given a sufficiently large order, $p$. The AR($p$) model may overfit other sources of variance, such as finite bias, thus it is desirable to keep $p$ as small as possible. Bayesian information criteria (BIC) may also be used to evaluate a set of models with varying $p$ to determine an optimal, minimized AR order \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978, vidaurre_survey_2013}.

Raising the order has a interpretive cost and it has limited the use of AR($p$) models in this literature. A timescale is a single number, and an AR($p$) fit returns a vector of $p$ coefficients. We show in the results section, that AR($p$) model is interpretable as a set of decaying and damped oscillatory processes, each with timescale.

\subsection{Oscillations}

In the context of neural data, timescales are typically estimated from the autocorrelation of LFPs or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. However both LFPs and single-unit spiking often exhibit rhythmic fluctuations -- oscillations. Oscillatory activity manifests as a cosine in the ACF \cite{brunel_fast_1999} or as a narrow peak in the PSD \cite{he_scale-free_2014, buzsaki_scaling_2013}. An AR(1) model has no term for either, so the timescale it reports absorbs the rhythm and is biased (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}).

Higher order models capture oscillations. An AR(2) process is a damped oscillator \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970}: a cosine in the ACF under an exponentially decaying envelope, and a peak of finite width in the PSD. Each further pair of coefficients adds another such rhythm, so an AR($p$) model describes the oscillations and the aperiodic decay together, and each rhythm is reported as a frequency and a timescale, the decay of its envelope. Neural rhythms wax and wane, drift in frequency, and often appear as brief bursts \cite{feingold_bursts_2015}, which is what a damped oscillator describes and what the empirical neural recordings show (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,b}).

The exception is a perfectly sustained sinusoid, such as line noise or a steady-state response. Its ACF is a cosine that never decays and its PSD is a line at a single frequency, which no AR model produces at any order. Such a rhythm should be excluded rather than modeled. This is possible in the PSD, where it occupies a few bins and can be discarded by robust regression or modeled explicitly as a peak \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}, and not in the ACF, where it perturbs every lag.

\subsubsection{Finite Sampling}

Stochastic processes suffer from statistical bias related to finite duration recorded signals (\figpanel{fig:bias}{c}). Decreasing the duration of a time series increases timescale estimation bias, limits the maximum detectable timescale, and decreases confidence. Moving towards unbiased timescale estimation approaches is necessary for studying the windows temporal dynamics of task designs (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Dynamic timescales have been observed in working memory \cite{wasmuht_intrinsic_2018, gao_neuronal_2020}, decision making \cite{ganupuru_flexibility_2019} and auditory processing \cite{chien_constructing_2020} tasks.

In the context of event-related task designs or neuronal spiking activity, bias is often mitigated by averaging the ACFs of trials or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In these approaches, when there are multiple neurons, channels, or repeat trials, it is recommended to take the median or mean PSD or ACF across recordings, neurons, and/or time periods, prior to model fitting. Averaging across neuronal populations, regions, and time has been shown to reduce estimation bias attributed to short duration recordings \cite{spitzner_mr_2021}. However, averaging limits the ability to examine between-neuron, region, or trial timescale variability. This suggests that across regions or neurons, signals should be stationary and from similar distributions.

\subsubsection{Non-stationarity}

A signal is stationary when its statistics do not change over time, and non-stationary otherwise. In the terms used here, a dynamic signal is one whose AR($p$) parameters, and therefore its timescale, are not constant across the recording. This occurs when a signal spans varying cognitive or neural states. Fit to such a signal, a single model combines distinct processes and reports a timescale that belongs to none of them (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Timescales should therefore be estimated from signals that are homogeneous with respect to anatomical or functional boundaries and to behavioral or task states. This can be done by windowing the recording into periods in which the state is constant, which requires choosing a window size and knowing which windows to combine, or by designing the experiment so that the state is held constant throughout. Windowing carries a cost, since shorter segments increase finite sampling bias and each estimate is less confident, leaving a trade-off between minimizing violations of stationarity and maximizing window size.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We first test the estimators on processes whose timescale is known (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{}): an AR(1) process, an AR(2) oscillator, and the AR(7) of \figpanel{fig:bias}{a}. Each is built with the same timescale, 200 ms. Six estimators are compared, three AR(1) and three AR($p$), fit to the signal, the ACF or the PSD, with the AR($p$) order chosen by BIC. \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} shows the two order-selected AR($p$) fits, to the ACF and to the PSD, and a fourth column in which the AR(1) is recorded for only twenty timescales. The AR(1) and time-domain estimators, and a scale-free process with no timescale at all, are reported in the Supplement.

When the process is AR(1), all six estimators recover the 200 ms timescale to within 5\%, order selection returns $p = 1$ in every domain, and the AR(1) model leaves under 1\% of the log spectrum unexplained. When the process is not AR(1), the same estimators disagree by up to a factor of 112, and every one of the disagreeing numbers is reproducible: repeated simulations return it to within a few percent. A reproducible timescale is not necessarily a correct one, and the two cannot be told apart without checking the model.

\subsubsection{Model Mismatch}\label{sec:modes}

The response to a signal that is not first order is to raise the order. The obstacle is that an AR($p$) fit returns $p$ coefficients rather than a timescale. The obstacle is removable, because the coefficients are not the natural description of the model.

Every AR($p$) model is equivalent to a small set of \emph{modes}, each one way the process rings down after an input: a decay with time constant $\tau$, and possibly a rhythm at frequency $f$ (\figpanel{fig:modes}{a}). The modes are the poles $\lambda_k$ of the model, the roots of its characteristic polynomial (Supplementary Methods). A real pole is a pure decay and a complex-conjugate pair is a damped oscillation, with
\begin{align}
\tau_k = \frac{-1/f_s}{\log|\lambda_k|}, \qquad f_k = \frac{f_s |\arg \lambda_k|}{2\pi}. \label{eq:mode}
\end{align}
For a real pole eq. \ref{eq:mode} reduces to eq. \ref{eq:tau}, so an AR(1) model is the special case of a single real pole. A conjugate pair adds a damped cosine to the ACF, $e^{-t/\tau_k}\cos(2\pi f_k t)$, in which $f_k$ sets the period and $\tau_k$ how fast the ringing dies away. The two are independent: a 10 Hz mode with $\tau = 150$ ms repeats every 100 ms and rings for 1.5 cycles before its envelope falls to $1/e$. An AR($p$) fit can therefore be reported as a short list of $(\tau, f)$ pairs. A real pole is one mode and a conjugate pair is also one mode, so the number of modes lies between $p/2$ and $p$.

The modes combine differently in the two domains, which is what makes a higher-order model hard to read by eye. In the ACF they add, one exponential decay per real pole and one damped cosine per conjugate pair (\figpanel{fig:modes}{c}). In the PSD they multiply, one factor per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{b}). Both decompositions are exact (Supplementary Methods). The decomposition is the autoregressive case of a general result for linear time series \cite{west_time_1997}, previously used to track the components of a fitted autoregression through EEG seizures \cite{west_evaluation_1999}. The use we put it to is reading a fitted AR($p$) as a set of neural timescales.

Both kinds of mode have a $\tau$, and they mean different things. For a real pole, $\tau$ is the decay of the autocorrelation, as in AR(1). For a conjugate pair, it is the decay of the envelope around the cosine, how fast the ringing dies away, not how long the process stays correlated. The product $f\tau$, the number of cycles a mode completes before its envelope decays by $1/e$, says whether it would be recognized as a rhythm; below one cycle the mode is a broad spectral shoulder rather than an oscillation. This is common in the recordings below, where four in ten rhythmic modes complete less than a cycle. Modes also differ in how much they matter. Deleting a mode and refitting the overall scale degrades the fitted spectrum by an amount with a closed form (Supplementary Methods), and that degradation is the mode's importance. Importance is measured on the log spectrum and is not a share of the variance: the 40 Hz mode of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} ranks second in importance and carries $2 \times 10^{-5}$ of the variance, so it is invisible in \figpanel{fig:modes}{c}. A rhythm can dominate the log spectrum and be absent from the autocorrelation.

The AR(7) of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} is built to be the case a single timescale cannot describe. It has five modes: relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms, and rhythms at 10 Hz with a 150 ms envelope and at 40 Hz with a 60 ms envelope. The two slower relaxations are of comparable importance and an order of magnitude apart, so no single relaxation time describes the process, and both rhythms ring for more than a cycle, so each envelope decay is distinct from its period. Ranked by importance, four of the five modes reproduce the spectrum to $R^2 = 0.997$; the 2 ms relaxation is the one left out.

The modes are recoverable from data in a way the coefficients are not. Across 200 independent 60-second realizations, the spectral AR($p$) fit recovered every mode, with median errors in $\tau$ of 8.0\%, 3.6\%, 2.5\%, 4.8\% and 1.9\% from the slowest mode to the fastest, and both rhythm frequencies to within 0.6\%. The first coefficient of the same fits was $5.92 \pm 0.01$: it exceeds one in every realization and has no timescale. An AR(1) spectral fit to the same signals ran to the 20 s upper bound of its search in every realization, which is the fit reporting that no single relaxation time describes the spectrum. In the benchmark (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, third column) the three AR($p$) estimators return 183 to 195 ms against a target of 200 ms, while the three AR(1) estimators spread over a factor of 112: 179 ms from the ACF, 1.57 s from the lagged regression, and the 20 s bound from the PSD.

\subsubsection{Oscillations}\label{sec:osc}

How an oscillation biases a timescale depends on whether the rhythm is part of the dynamics or added to them. The AR(2) oscillator of \figpanel{fig:bias}{b} is a rhythm that drifts in phase and amplitude, which is what neural rhythms look like \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970, feingold_bursts_2015}, and it is exactly autoregressive. Here raising the order works in both domains (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, second column). The AR($p$) fits recover the 200 ms envelope decay to within 1\% and the 10 Hz rhythm to within 0.01 Hz, while the AR(1) fits return 10, 139 and 251 ms, depending only on the domain they were fit in. Across a decade of envelope decays and three octaves of frequency (Supplementary Methods), the AR($p$) spectral fit stays within 3\% of the envelope decay and the AR($p$) ACF fit within 9\%, while the AR(1) fits are wrong by factors between 0.016 and 133 that depend on both the decay and the frequency. A rhythm that is part of the dynamics should be modeled. Modeling it returns its frequency and its envelope decay, which an AR(1) fit conflates with the aperiodic timescale.

A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is different. It is not autoregressive at any order: its spectrum contains a line that no product of poles reproduces, and its ACF contains a cosine that never decays. In the ACF this is fatal (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). As the rhythm grows from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, the AR(1) ACF fit falls from the true 50 ms to as little as 3 ms, and raising the order changes the failure without removing it: the AR($p$) ACF fit ranges from half to twelve times the truth over the same grid. The reason is structural. The weight of each term in an AR($p$) autocorrelation is set by the poles rather than fit freely, and a mixture of an exponential and an undamped cosine has a weight of its own that no set of poles reproduces. In the PSD the rhythm occupies a few bins, robust regression discards them, and both spectral fits stay within 3\% of the truth at every frequency and power tested. An added rhythm can only be avoided, and the spectrum is where it can be avoided. The solution to oscillatory bias is therefore to raise the AR order, to fit the spectrum robustly, or both, depending on which kind of rhythm the signal contains.

\subsubsection{Finite Samples}\label{sec:finite}

A short recording biases a timescale and makes it imprecise, and both effects depend on the recording only through the ratio of its duration $T$ to the timescale $\tau$. Propagating the classical bias and variance of the AR(1) coefficient \cite{mann_statistical_1943, marriott_bias_1954} through eq. \ref{eq:tau} gives a rule of thumb (Supplementary Methods): the relative bias is about $-2\tau/T$ and the relative standard error about $\sqrt{2\tau/T}$. A bias under 10\% therefore needs about $20\tau$ of data and a standard error under 10\% about $200\tau$, whatever the sampling rate and whatever the value of $\tau$. For a 100 ms cortical timescale that is 2 and 20 seconds of stationary data per estimate. Precision improves only as the square root of duration, so halving the error costs four times the data. The sampling distribution of $\hat\tau$ is skewed to the right (\figpanel{fig:finite}{c}), because eq. \ref{eq:tau} is convex in $\varphi$: a short recording more often underestimates the timescale, but errs further when it overestimates. The last column of \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} shows the same effect for the AR($p$) fits. On four seconds of the 200 ms AR(1), twenty timescales, the median estimates fall to 144 and 152 ms and the central 80\% of estimates spans a factor of five.

When there is no timescale to recover, a short window does something worse than add noise. On a scale-free process, the ACF fit returns an estimate proportional to the length of the recording over three decades of length, while the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat. A number obtained this way describes the analysis window, not the signal.

This is what makes non-stationarity the hardest of the four biases. The response to a timescale that changes with state is to shorten the window to the state, and a window short relative to the timescale is exactly the one that biases the estimate. Two responses are available. The first is to accept short windows and the bias they carry, correcting for it and averaging across trials where the design allows. The second, and the better one, is to design the experiment so that the state of interest lasts long enough, tens to hundreds of timescales, for a single window to be both stationary and precise.

\subsection{Empirical}\label{sec:realdata}

Simulations show what can go wrong; recordings show how often it does. We applied one identical pipeline to eight publicly available recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity in humans, macaque, mouse and rat, with no dataset-specific artifact rejection, filtering or oscillation removal (Supplementary Methods). The aim is not a biological claim about any one recording but a count of how often the single-timescale model is adequate when the same question is put to signals of very different origin.

Timescales span species, modalities and brain regions, and the estimates reproduce the scale separation that motivates the hierarchy literature. Field-potential timescales were tens of milliseconds, between 11 and 52 ms by the AR(1) spectral fit; binned spike counts gave 232 ms; and precuneus BOLD gave 1.0 to 6.6 s. The gap of three orders of magnitude between hemodynamic and electrophysiological estimates is expected, and it is a reminder that comparing timescales across modalities compares different models fit to differently filtered data \cite{raut_organization_2020}.

No recording was AR(1). Every field-potential recording contained a narrowband peak, and the AR($p$) fit placed a rhythmic mode inside the expected band in six of the eight; the exceptions, resting-state fMRI and the spike counts, have no narrowband rhythm to find. In six of the eight the empirical ACF oscillates rather than decaying, and only the AR($p$) model follows it (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,c}). The mismatch is measurable. An AR(1) model left between 5\% and 41\% of the log spectrum unexplained in the field potentials, 24\% in fMRI and 46\% in the spike counts, with a median of 14\% across the eight; an order-selected AR($p$) model reduced this to between 0.2\% and 3\% in the field potentials, 21\% in fMRI and 38\% in the spike counts, with a median of 1.3\%. The spike counts are the hardest case because a spike train adds a flat Poisson floor that the AR spectral model has no term for, and we fit them only where the process rises above that floor (Supplementary Methods).

Because no recording satisfied the AR(1) assumption, the estimators disagreed. Across the AR(1) and AR($p$) fits in the ACF and the PSD, the ratio of the largest reported timescale to the smallest ranged from 1.3 to 13 with a median of 6.1; including the two time-domain fits it ranged from 1.3 to 179 with a median of 11.7. The largest disagreements were in the recordings with the strongest rhythms, motor ECoG, posterior alpha and mouse LFP, where the AR(1) fits report a fast decay and the AR($p$) fits a slower relaxation once the rhythm is described rather than absorbed. The most extreme single case is a lagged regression on sensorimotor MEG that returned 2.0 s against 11 ms from the ACF and 22 ms from the PSD, reading a sustained beta rhythm as persistence. The smallest disagreements, under 1.5-fold, were in resting-state fMRI and macaque ECoG, the two recordings whose spectra have the least structure to disagree about.

The modes say what each recording contains (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b,d,e}). Decomposing each spectral AR($p$) fit, and keeping only the modes whose timescale the analysis band can resolve (Supplementary Methods), leaves between one and twelve modes per trace, with a median of seven or more in every field potential, of which the figure shows the three most important. Only resting-state fMRI is carried by a single component, a 2.6 s relaxation in every trace. Every other recording needs two or more, and the second is not a detail. In posterior alpha the largest component is a 113 ms rhythm at 10 Hz, the second a 24 ms rhythm at 21 Hz and the third a 46 ms relaxation; in rat hippocampus an 88 ms rhythm at 9 Hz, a 46 ms rhythm at 21 Hz and a 76 ms relaxation; in sensorimotor MEG a 26 ms rhythm at 16 Hz, a 40 ms relaxation and an 18 ms rhythm at 28 Hz; in macaque ECoG a 45 ms rhythm at 15 Hz beside a 126 ms relaxation. In each of these a single reported $\tau$ is not a compromise between the numbers but a choice of one of them, made by the estimator. Identifying the dominant relaxation mode, and reporting the rhythm beside it, is what a higher-order model provides that a single timescale cannot.

Cutting the recordings into windows shows the fourth bias (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Measured in units of the timescale, the median estimate settled to within 20\% of its long-window value only once windows exceeded a few hundred timescales, and at the shortest windows it fell to between a sixth and three quarters of that value. The spread across windows of a given length is a separate matter. If finite sampling were the only source of variability, that spread would follow the AR(1) sampling bound. At the longest windows available it exceeded the bound in all eight recordings, by a median factor of about seven and by up to thirty. At the durations where an estimate becomes precise enough to report, the dominant source of variability is change in the signal over time, and a timescale reported for a long recording is an average over a quantity that was changing throughout it.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

A neural timescale is a parameter of a model. When the model is the AR(1) process, as it is for an exponential fit to the ACF, a Lorentzian fit to the PSD, or a lagged regression on the signal, $\tau$ is the relaxation time of that process, and reporting it as a property of a region, a state or a subject is reasonable. When the model is wrong, $\tau$ is still well defined, still precise, still reproducible and still comparable across conditions, but it is no longer the quantity the interpretation is about. Our simulations show that the two situations look identical from the outside, and our recordings show that neural data are usually in the second one. Three checks tell them apart at no cost: agreement between independent estimators, which is within a few percent when the data are AR(1) and up to 112-fold in simulation and 179-fold in the recordings when they are not; the fraction of the spectrum or ACF the fitted model leaves unexplained; and the order BIC selects. None of the eight recordings passed.

The four biases call for different responses. Model mismatch calls for a higher order, and the resulting model is readable as a list of decay times and rhythms, of which the dominant relaxation mode is the timescale an AR(1) fit was trying to report. An oscillation that is part of the dynamics is an AR(2) process and is best modeled, which recovers its frequency and its envelope decay; an oscillation added to the signal, such as line noise or a steady-state response, is not autoregressive at any order and must be avoided, which is possible in the PSD and not in the ACF. Finite samples set a floor of about $200\tau$ per estimate, and non-stationarity pushes against that floor: a window short enough to be stationary is often too short to be precise, and the resolution is either to average short windows across trials or to design experiments whose states last long enough to be estimated. In the recordings, the spread of estimates across windows was about an order of magnitude larger than finite sampling predicts, so a confidence interval computed from the sampling formula understates the uncertainty of a windowed estimate, and one computed from the spread across windows does not. The biases rarely occur alone. Every recording here had a rhythm, a finite duration and a timescale that drifted, and they have to be handled together.

\subsection{Recommendations}

We suggest the following when reporting a neural timescale. \textit{First}, state the model rather than only the estimator, for example ``the relaxation time of an AR(1) fit to the 1-100 Hz spectrum'' rather than ``the neural timescale''. \textit{Second}, report the ratio of recording length to estimated timescale, and treat estimates below roughly $200\tau$ as precision-limited. \textit{Third}, report the fraction of the spectrum or autocorrelation left unexplained by the fitted model. \textit{Fourth}, report at least one independent estimator; disagreement beyond about 1.5-fold is evidence of model mismatch rather than of noise. \textit{Fifth}, if the data contain a strong narrowband rhythm, prefer spectral-domain estimation and state whether the rhythm is being modeled or excluded. \textit{Sixth}, if an AR($p$) model is used, report the modes as $(\tau, f)$ pairs with their importances and the number needed to reproduce the fit, not the coefficient vector. \textit{Finally}, when windowing, take the confidence interval from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our simulations treat stationary processes, and non-stationarity is addressed only through windowing; it remains the hardest of the four biases because shortening windows to reduce it increases finite sampling bias. Seven of the eight recordings are field potentials or a hemodynamic signal, and only one is a spike train, for which binning and Poisson variability add a flat floor that the AR spectral model cannot represent \cite{zeraati_flexible_2022, meyer_inferring_2019}; we handle it only by restricting the fit band, and a model with an explicit observation-noise term would treat it properly. The real-data analysis deliberately applies one pipeline to every recording, so the preprocessing a specialist would apply, artifact rejection, burst detection, oscillation removal, is absent by design, and the mismatch we report is an upper bound on what careful per-dataset treatment would leave. Finally, the mode decomposition is exact for the fitted model but inherits its assumptions. It is a parsimonious description of a spectrum, and whether a given mode corresponds to a separable biological process is a question the model cannot answer: the sum of two independent AR(1) processes is not itself autoregressive, and its fitted poles are pulled away from both original timescales.

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:smethods}

\subsubsection*{Conventions}

We write the AR($p$) model as in eq. \ref{eq:arp}, so that the characteristic polynomial is $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$ and stationarity requires all of its roots to lie inside the unit circle. Sign conventions differ between texts and the two common polynomials are reciprocals of one another; we use this one throughout, so that ``pole inside the unit circle'' means ``stationary''. The one-sided spectral density is
\begin{align}
S(f) = \frac{2\sigma^2/f_s}{\left|1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\varphi_k e^{-i 2\pi f k/f_s}\right|^{2}}, \qquad 0 \le f \le f_s/2,
\end{align}
normalized so that its integral is the process variance, matching Welch's method \cite{welch_use_1967}. For $p=1$ this is the discrete-time Lorentzian whose knee frequency is $1/(2\pi\tau)$. The theoretical autocovariance is obtained by solving the Yule-Walker system for the first $p+1$ lags and recursing thereafter.

\subsubsection*{Estimators}

Six estimators were compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in and in the model order, and otherwise share everything: each consumes the same mean-centered signal and the same Welch power spectrum \cite{welch_use_1967}, so that differences between them cannot be attributed to preprocessing. In the time domain, the AR(1) estimator is a least-squares regression of $x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$, and the AR($p$) estimator uses Burg's method \cite{burg_maximum_1975}. In the autocorrelation domain, the model is $A\,\rho_p(k) + B$, where $\rho_p$ is the exact AR($p$) autocorrelation, $A$ absorbs a white-noise floor at lag zero and $B$ a constant offset; for $p=1$ this is the exponential-plus-offset model standard in this literature \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In the spectral domain, the model is eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} with a free scale, fit to $\log_{10}$ power, the convention of spectral parameterization methods \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}.

Making the sharing exact requires care with the autocorrelation. A spectral fit is restricted to an analysis band, 1-100 Hz for most of the electrophysiological recordings here, whereas the sample autocorrelation of the raw signal contains everything in the recording, including drift below the band. Fitting one estimator to the band and the other to the whole signal compares two answers to two different questions. We therefore compute the autocorrelation from the same band-limited spectrum the spectral estimators see, by inverse Fourier transform, with bins excluded as line noise filled by log-linear interpolation from their neighbours rather than zeroed, so that notching does not ring in the lag domain. The difference is large: on rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 the raw autocorrelation gives an AR(1) timescale of 938 ms, set by slow drift the spectral fit never saw, against 14 ms once both estimators describe the same band. One further correction is needed. Welch's estimate is the average periodogram of tapered segments, so the autocovariance it implies is the true autocovariance multiplied by the taper's own autocorrelation, which for a Hann window has fallen to 0.66 at a lag one quarter of the segment length. Left in place this taper masquerades as a faster decay, so we divide it out and discard the lags at which the taper retains less than a fifth of its energy. A model fit to the spectrum is carried into the lag domain by the same route, its spectrum evaluated on the same grid, restricted to the same band and inverse transformed, rather than by its analytic full-band autocorrelation, which would carry the model's extrapolation outside the band (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{c}). The components of \figpanel{fig:realdata}{d} are the partial fractions of the fitted spectrum, one term $A_j(1-\lambda_j^2)/(1 - 2\lambda_j\cos\omega + \lambda_j^2)$ per pole, transformed the same way; they add exactly to the model's band-limited autocorrelation.

Robust (soft-L1) regression is used in the spectral domain only, and only when the signal contains a rhythm: in simulation, for the AR(2) oscillator and for the added sinusoid of the sweep; in real data, for every recording, since all of them contain a narrowband peak. A rhythm is an outlier in the spectrum, occupying a handful of bins, and can be down-weighted there; in the lag domain it perturbs every lag, so down-weighting large residuals discards the fit rather than the rhythm. Robustness is not free: when there is no outlier to reject, down-weighting the tails of an ordinary residual distribution biases the fit, so it is a claim about the data and not a default. It also trades one thing a fit can report for another. On an AR(1) background at $\tau = 50$ ms plus a broadband AR(2) rhythm at 10 Hz, the robust spectral fit recovered the aperiodic timescale at 53 ms over 20 realizations while discounting the rhythm; without robustness the fit found the rhythm in all 20 realizations at a median of 9.4 Hz and returned the aperiodic timescale at 68 ms. A study that wants both should report them from two fits and say so.

Both the autocorrelation and spectral fits are optimized over reflection coefficients rather than over the AR coefficients directly. Every point in the box $|k_i| < 1$ is a stationary model, the parameters are all of order one, and the fit cannot wander into a region where mode timescales are undefined; without this reparameterization 48\% of the spectral fits to real data returned non-stationary parameters. Multiple starting points are used because the objective is non-convex when a rhythm is present.

Two further requirements are imposed on the fitted model, because the model is being used as an instrument for measuring a timescale rather than only as a description of the data. First, the model must contain a relaxation mode. Nothing in a least-squares fit requires this: a converged, well-fitting AR($p$) can place every pole in a conjugate pair, in which case it has no decay time to report, and before the constraint a third of the fits to our recordings did exactly that. We therefore factor one real pole out of the characteristic polynomial, $A(z^{-1}) = (1 - a z^{-1})A_{\mathrm{rest}}(z^{-1})$ with $0 < a < 1$, and parameterize the remainder by reflection coefficients, so that a relaxation mode exists by construction. The requirement costs one order and is applied at every order, including two; on an AR(2) oscillator BIC then selects order three. The constraint costs essentially nothing in fit: across our recordings the median change in each estimator's own objective was $+0.003$ for the lag-domain fit and $-0.000$ for the spectral fit. Second, the reported aperiodic timescale is the most important \emph{non-rhythmic} mode, where non-rhythmic means $f\tau < 1$, and a real pole qualifies trivially. A conjugate pair that rings for a third of a cycle is a damped relaxation, and the guaranteed real pole is guaranteed to exist, not to matter: on mouse LFP the constrained spectral fit parks its required pole at 0.6 ms with negligible importance while the aperiodic structure sits in an 88 ms pair ringing 0.57 of a cycle. Importance, not pole type, decides.

Spectra are estimated by Welch's method, except for fMRI. A 296-sample record cannot afford Welch's trade: segmenting it leaves either too few frequency bins to fit or too few segments to average. We therefore use a multitaper estimate of the whole record \cite{thomson_spectrum_1982}, with time-bandwidth product $NW = 5$, and thin its bins to the resolution bandwidth before fitting, because adjacent multitaper bins inside that bandwidth are not independent; leaving them in makes the record look eight times larger than it is, and order selection then buys orders it cannot support.

Models fit in either domain are scored in both. Spectral fit is the coefficient of determination of $\log_{10}$ power over the analysis band, with the overall scale refit; autocorrelation fit is the coefficient of determination over the fitted lags, with $A$ and $B$ refit.

\subsubsection*{Model order}

Order is selected by BIC \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978} in the domain the model is fit in: from the conditional Gaussian likelihood in the time domain, and from the residual sum of squares in the autocorrelation and spectral domains. The search runs over a log-spaced grid of orders up to 20. In the lag domain, neighbouring lags are strongly dependent, so the criterion uses an effective sample size, the number of lags divided by the lag at which the autocorrelation first falls below $1/e$. When the selected order reaches the top of the range this is recorded and reported, because it indicates a process the AR class cannot represent at any practical order rather than a well-determined order.

\subsubsection*{Modes}

The AR($p$) recursion in eq. \ref{eq:arp} has a characteristic polynomial $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, whose roots $\lambda_k$ we call the poles. Stationarity requires $|\lambda_k| < 1$. Dividing by $z^p$ gives
\begin{align}
1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} \varphi_k z^{-k} = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (1 - \lambda_k z^{-1}), \label{eq:factor}
\end{align}
so the spectral denominator in eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} factorizes into first-order terms and each pole contributes a decay time and, when complex, a frequency (eq. \ref{eq:mode}). Because the coefficients are real, complex poles come in conjugate pairs, and we combine each pair into a single damped oscillatory mode equivalent to a real AR(2) factor. A real pole is a pure relaxation mode. The term is borrowed from linear systems theory, where the modes of a linear system are the eigenvalues that govern how it relaxes.

Two points about this factorization are easy to state wrongly. It is a factorization of the transfer function, not a decomposition of the signal into independent AR(1) processes: the factors multiply in the spectrum, and an AR($p$) process is a cascade of first-order filters driven by one input. Conversely, the same poles do appear additively in the time domain. The impulse response is $\psi_n = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^n$, so the response to an input is a sum of decaying modes driven by a shared innovation, and the autocovariance decomposes the same way,
\begin{align}
\gamma(k) = \sum_j A_j \lambda_j^{\,k}. \label{eq:acfmodes}
\end{align}
Grouping the conjugate terms, the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of exponential decays and damped cosines, one per mode. The product form and the sum form are the factored and partial-fraction representations of the same rational function, and they are not transforms of each other: a product in the frequency domain is a convolution in the lag domain, not a sum. What the partial-fraction form costs is separability: each residue $A_j$ depends on every pole, so a mode's contribution to the autocorrelation is not a property of that mode alone. This is also why a lag-domain AR($p$) fit to an added sinusoid is biased (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}): the residues are not free parameters, so the model has no parameter for the mixing weight of an external mixture. Because the modes share an input, the process variance does not decompose into per-mode shares. In our implementation both decompositions reproduce the model to better than one part in $10^7$.

\subsubsection*{Mode importance}

Writing $g_k(f)$ for the log of the $k$th pole factor, the model log spectrum is $\log \sigma^2 - \sum_k g_k(f)$: modes are additive in log power. Deleting mode $k$ and refitting the overall scale by least squares therefore leaves a residual whose root-mean-square in $\log_{10}$ power is exactly $\mathrm{sd}_f\{g_k\}/\ln 10$, and we use this as the mode's importance. An Itakura-Saito variant, equal to the asymptotic Whittle log-likelihood degradation \cite{whittle_estimation_1953, itakura_analysis_1968}, gives the same ranking in every case we examined. The error of a model retaining an arbitrary subset of modes is a quadratic form in the retained set, so the best subset of each size can be evaluated in closed form; the dominant modes are the smallest subset reproducing the fitted log spectrum with $R^2 \ge 0.99$.

For the real recordings, a mode is reported only if the analysis band can resolve its timescale. A mode slower than $1/(2\pi f_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $f_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the lower edge of the fitted band, has its knee below the band, so its timescale is an extrapolation of the model past the data rather than a measurement; left in, these near-unit-root poles report timescales longer than the recording and carry high importance precisely because deleting them changes the lowest frequencies. The criterion discards 8\% of the fitted spectral modes. Likewise a rhythmic mode counts as detected only if its frequency lies inside the fitted range.

\subsubsection*{Finite-sample theory}

For a mean-corrected least-squares AR(1) fit on $n$ samples, the classical results are $\mathbb{E}[\hat\varphi] - \varphi \approx -(1+3\varphi)/n$ \cite{marriott_bias_1954, kendall_note_1954} and
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[ (\hat{\varphi} - \varphi)^2 ] &= \frac{1 - \varphi^2}{n-1}. \label{eq:var}
\end{align}
Propagating both through eq. \ref{eq:tau} to second order, and writing $T = n/f_s$ for the recording duration, gives in the regime $\tau f_s \gg 1$ that all neural recordings occupy
\begin{align}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] - \tau}{\tau} \approx -\frac{2\tau}{T}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}[\hat\tau]}{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{T}}. \label{eq:finite}
\end{align}
Both depend on the recording only through $T/\tau$, and both were verified against Monte Carlo. This is why estimation error is reported throughout as the ratio $\hat\tau/\tau$ rather than as $|\hat\tau - \tau|$ or $(\hat\tau - \tau)^2$: the ratio has the same distribution for a 10 ms and a 1 s timescale at the same $T/\tau$, whereas the absolute and squared errors scale with $\tau$ and $\tau^2$. Confidence intervals are obtained by mapping the interval for $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau}, which preserves coverage because the map is monotone. The intervals of \figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b} are the least-squares intervals of eq. \ref{eq:var} evaluated at each estimate and averaged over simulations; the weighted least-squares/Cauchy construction \cite{gallagher_small_2008} refines them for $\varphi$ very close to one.

\subsubsection*{The integrated timescale}

As a model-free reference we use $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^\infty \rho(t)\,dt$ \cite{sokal_monte_1997, priestley_spectral_1981}, evaluated with the trapezoid convention so that a sampled exponential returns its continuous-time $\tau$ rather than $\tau + 1/(2f_s)$. Equivalently $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = S(0)/(4\gamma(0))$. It equals $\tau$ for an AR(1) process, is the variance-weighted mean of component timescales for a sum of independent AR(1) processes, and diverges for a long-memory process. It is not the envelope decay time of an oscillator: for a damped oscillator the anticorrelated troughs of the autocorrelation cancel most of the integral, which is why the AR(2) oscillator of the benchmark has $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = 2.5$ ms and an envelope decay of 200 ms. For an oscillatory process, how long activity stays correlated and how fast a perturbation decays are different questions with different answers.

\subsubsection*{Simulated processes}

Four processes were used in the benchmark, each with a closed-form spectrum and autocorrelation, simulated at 500 Hz for 100 s with 200 repetitions. Each isolates one way the AR(1) assumption can fail, and every process with a timescale has the same one, 200 ms, so that the estimates can be read on one axis. \textit{AR(1)} with $\tau = 200$ ms is the positive control: the model is correct, so an estimator that fails here fails for reasons of its own. \textit{AR(2) oscillator} has poles at $|\lambda| e^{\pm i\theta}$ with $|\lambda| = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}$ and $\theta = 2\pi f_0/f_s$, here $f_0 = 10$ Hz and $\tau = 200$ ms, two cycles; it is a rhythm the model represents exactly. \textit{AR($p$)} is the AR(7) of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} and of the model-mismatch column of \figpanel{fig:bias}{}: relaxations of 200, 20 and 2 ms and rhythms at 10 Hz ringing for 150 ms and at 40 Hz for 60 ms. It is the only process inside the model family but above first order, which is what makes it necessary: every other failing process confounds model mismatch with order mismatch, and this one isolates the second. Its two slow relaxations are of comparable importance an order of magnitude apart, so that no single timescale describes it, and its rhythms ring for more than a cycle, so that the rule an estimator is asked to apply, the most important non-rhythmic mode, has an unambiguous target, the 200 ms relaxation. \textit{Power law} is a long-memory ARFIMA process \cite{granger_introduction_1980, hosking_fractional_1981} simulated exactly by circulant embedding \cite{davies_tests_1987}; it is outside the family and has no characteristic timescale, so there is nothing for an estimator to recover. The finite-sample column of \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} is the same AR(1) recorded for four seconds, twenty timescales, with the Welch window and the autocorrelation a quarter of the recording, as in the windowing analysis below. An alternative higher-order process, an AR(5) with a 250 ms relaxation, a 5 Hz mode ringing half a cycle and a 50 Hz rhythm ringing one cycle, is simulated alongside and can be drawn in place of the AR(7) in every figure; it is not shown.

The rhythm that must be avoided rather than modeled, \textit{AR(1) plus sinusoid}, adds $A\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)$ with random phase to an AR(1) with $\tau = 50$ ms, parameterized by the ratio of oscillatory to aperiodic variance. It is swept over six frequencies from 4 to 40 Hz and six power ratios from one eighth to four, with 20 repetitions per cell (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). The damped-oscillator sweep of the main text takes the AR(2) oscillator over envelope decays of 20, 50 and 150 ms and frequencies of 5, 10, 20 and 40 Hz, with 20 repetitions per cell.

Outside AR(1) the target is not given and has to be stated. We take it to be whatever the constructed process gives under the same rule the estimator is asked to apply, computed from the true poles: the relaxation time for AR(1), the envelope decay for the AR(2) oscillator, and for the AR(7) the most important non-rhythmic mode, which is its 200 ms relaxation. The rule and the construction can disagree, since a rhythm ringing less than a cycle is non-rhythmic by the rule; in the alternative AR(5) they do disagree, and its target is the 100 ms envelope of its 5 Hz mode rather than its 250 ms relaxation. Every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation was verified against long simulations.

\subsubsection*{Benchmark results}

When the process is AR(1), all six estimators recover the 200 ms timescale to within 5\% (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, first column). For the AR(2) oscillator the three AR(1) estimators return 10, 139 and 251 ms for a 200 ms envelope decay, while all three AR($p$) fits recover it to within 1.1\% and the rhythm to within 0.01 Hz. For the AR(7) the three AR($p$) estimators return 183 to 195 ms against a target of 200 ms, while the three AR(1) estimators spread over a factor of 112: 179 ms for the autocorrelation fit, which the 200 ms relaxation happens to dominate, 1.57 s for the lagged regression, and the 20 s bound of its search for the spectral fit. The long-memory process produces estimates from 4 to 86 ms, a twenty-fold range, every one of them stable across repetitions.

Model mismatch tracks these failures. An AR(1) spectral fit leaves 0.7\% of the log spectrum unexplained for the AR(1) process, but 27\% for the AR(2) oscillator, 67\% for the AR(7) and 26\% for the power law; AR($p$) reduces these to between 0.03\% and 4.0\%. BIC returns $p=1$ for AR(1), $p=3$ for the AR(2) oscillator (its true order plus the required real pole), $p=8$ for the AR(7), one above its true order, and runs to the top of the search range for the power law. Fitting each process's exact autocorrelation, which is what any consistent estimator converges to with infinite data, the mismatch collapses to zero at the correct order for the three autoregressive processes and only shrinks, without vanishing, for the power law, which no finite order removes.

The domain a model is fit in changes the model. Across the three processes that are not AR(1), fits to the spectrum describe the autocorrelation nearly as well as fits to the autocorrelation do, leaving between 0.2\% and 7\% of it unexplained (medians over repetitions). Fits to the autocorrelation describe the spectrum badly, leaving 15\% unexplained for the AR(2) oscillator, 11\% for the AR(7), and for the long-memory process more than the total spectral variance; on one realization of the long-memory process, the order-five model chosen in the lag domain has a spectrum spanning eleven orders of magnitude across the analysis band, where the data span two. Both domains use the same model, reparameterization, order selection and band, so this is not an optimization artifact. A model estimated in the frequency domain is a usable description of the signal in either domain; a model estimated in the lag domain is not. On a scale-free process the lag-domain fit also returns an estimate proportional to the recording length, $\hat\tau \propto T$ over three decades, where the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and processing}

Eight publicly available recordings were used, selected to span species, recording modality and oscillatory regime rather than to address any single biological question. \textit{fMRI}: ABIDE resting-state BOLD \cite{di_martino_autism_2014}, six participants from the UM site, preprocessed by C-PAC without temporal filtering, TR 2 s. We take the precuneus, left and right, giving twelve traces. A single 296-sample BOLD run gives a spectrum too noisy to fit, and averaging over regions is the obvious remedy but the wrong one: regions differ in exactly the property being measured, so an average over all 111 Harvard-Oxford regions would describe no region. One default-mode hub keeps the average interpretable and makes a trace here the same kind of object as a channel elsewhere. The unfiltered pipeline is used deliberately, since the usual 0.01-0.1 Hz band-pass removes most of the spectrum a timescale is estimated from. \textit{MEG}: the FieldTrip cortico-muscular coherence recording \cite{schoffelen_neuronal_2005, oostenveld_fieldtrip_2011}, eight sensorimotor magnetometers during isometric contraction. \textit{EEG}: MPI-LEMON eyes-closed rest \cite{babayan_mind_2019}, posterior channels. \textit{ECoG}: pre-DBS primary motor cortex in Parkinson disease \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}; and macaque visual cortex during a grating task \cite{nagasaka_multidimensional_2011}. \textit{LFP}: mouse Neuropixels in VISam during natural movie viewing \cite{siegle_survey_2021}; and rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 \cite{mizuseki_theta_2009, mizuseki_hc3_2013}. \textit{Spiking}: spike counts of eight VISp units from the same Neuropixels session \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, binned at 50 ms over the same 300 s of natural movie. The neural-timescale literature rests largely on spike-count autocorrelations, so this is the one modality whose absence would be conspicuous.

Electrophysiological recordings were resampled to 250 Hz and fit over 1-100 Hz, with line-noise fundamentals and harmonics excluded from the spectral fits; the MEG is fit over 1-95 Hz because its spectrum carries a sharp peak at 100 Hz that is an artefact, not brain. Power spectra were computed with Welch's method, 4 s windows and 50\% overlap. fMRI carries its own band, 0.01-0.24 Hz, because it is sampled three orders of magnitude more slowly. The spike counts are fit over 0.05-2 Hz rather than the 0.05-8 Hz their sampling rate allows: a spike train contributes a flat Poisson floor on top of whatever the membrane dynamics contribute, the AR spectral model $\sigma^2/|A|^2$ has no additive term for a floor, and above about 1.5 Hz the binned-count spectrum is within a factor of 1.5 of its own floor. Fitted over the full band the AR(1) model was fitting mostly shot noise, leaving 95\% of the log spectrum unexplained; restricting the fit to where the process is still above its shot noise improves it tenfold and brings the two spectral estimates from disagreeing by a factor of eleven to a factor of two. The band over which a spike-count model is fitted is a modelling choice that changes the answer and should be reported. No other filtering, artifact rejection or oscillation removal was applied.

\subsubsection*{Windowing real recordings}

Each recording was cut into non-overlapping windows of increasing length, from 0.5 s upward, and every window analysed with the identical pipeline (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Window length is measured in units of the timescale being estimated rather than in seconds, since a 1 s window is generous for a 4 ms timescale and hopeless for a 1 s one. Measured this way, the median spectral AR($p$) estimate settled to within 20\% of its longest-window value once the window exceeded between 60 and 1200 timescales, depending on the recording, with a median of about 340; at the shortest windows it sat between 15\% and 100\% of that value. Taking the autocorrelation from the raw signal instead of from the analysis band makes this far worse: estimates collapse by up to two orders of magnitude at the shortest windows and climb steadily with window length, which reads as a window-length effect on the brain's timescales and is instead drift below the fit band entering the lag domain. An artifact of this shape is easy to produce by accident and hard to notice.

The spread between windows is a different matter. If finite sampling were the only source of variability, the spread of estimates across windows of a given length would follow the AR(1) sampling bound of eq. \ref{eq:finite}. At the longest windows available, every estimator exceeded that bound in all eight recordings, by a median factor of between 6 and 8 and by up to 30. This does not go away when the autocorrelation is corrected. At the durations where an estimate becomes precise enough to report, the dominant source of variability is change in the signal over time.

\subsubsection*{Code}

All analyses use an open-source Python module with 200 unit tests covering, among other things, exact reconstruction of the AR($p$) spectrum and autocorrelation from the mode decomposition, agreement between the closed-form mode importance and a brute-force deletion computation, agreement of every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation with long simulations, the guaranteed relaxation mode, and agreement of eq. \ref{eq:finite} with Monte Carlo. Every figure is drawn by its own script in \texttt{revision/scripts/figures/} from the cached result tables, and the simulation stages, tables and figures are tracked as one reproducible pipeline. Code, figure scripts and result tables are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}.
