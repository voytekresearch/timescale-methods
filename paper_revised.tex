\abstract{
In our collective attempt to understand the ``neural code'', we have developed many conceptual and mathematical tools for deciphering neuronal activity, from analyses of firing rates to those of spike timing and the interdependence of spike times relative to one another. In sensory cortices, for example, all forms of neural activity, from spiking to EEG, can reliably predict future neural activity up to a few hundred milliseconds later, after which neural activity is more independent. In contrast, activity in association cortex predicts subsequent activity up to several seconds later. This temporal dependency of neural activity is often captured under the conceptual umbrella of ``neural timescales'', a concept that has received a lot of recent attention because signals with longer timescales are argued to have a longer ``memory'', making timescales attractive for neuroscience applications. While intuitive, there are many different descriptive mathematical models for quantifying timescales, such as extracting a decay parameter from the autocorrelation function or fitting Lorentzian functions to power spectra, and each of them is the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model written in a different domain. Here, we show in both simulated and real neural data how common neural signal features -- model mismatch, neural oscillations, the finite duration of our signals, and dynamic timescales -- can yield inaccurate or uninterpretable timescale estimates under traditional timescale quantification methods. We show that higher-order autoregressive, AR($p$), models, which are designed to explicitly predict future data from past time points, handle these issues, and that an AR($p$) fit can be read directly as a short list of decay times and rhythms, its modes, rather than as a list of coefficients. Applied to eight recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity in four species, the AR(1) model was inadequate in every recording and six of the eight required two or more modes. We provide recommendations for reporting neural timescales in a way that makes these assumptions visible.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Timescales}\label{sec:timescales}

Many real world processes are correlated at varying scales. For example, when trying to predict tomorrow's high temperature, a reasonable starting point is to use the high temperature from today. This also works to predict the high temperature the day after, and the day after that, and so on. One common approach for capturing these temporal relationships is through the use of an autocorrelation, which quantifies how correlated a signal is with itself at every temporal lag. The autocorrelation function (ACF) describes the resulting curve, which can be visualized as the correlation at each time lag (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}).

At very short lags, the correlation will be much higher, and the farther out in time you get, the lower the correlations become. The rate at which the ACF decays from correlated to uncorrelated is usually described as the ``timescale'' of the process. For purely random, Gaussian distributed, white noise processes, the timescale is very ``fast'' -- dropping from perfectly correlated at lag-zero to uncorrelated at all other lags. Timescales are often quantified as the exponential decay rate of the ACF, where numerous real-world processes, ranging from the atomic scale with radioactive decay \cite{semkow_oscillations_2009}, to the astronomical with stellar flares \cite{anfinogentov_decaying_2013}, show temporal dependence. Regardless of the domain or modality, the mathematical basis of timescales is the same, while interpretations and ground truth mechanisms will vary based on the signal's context.

\subsection{Neural Timescales}\label{sec:neural}

In the context of neuroscience, timescales have been observed across recording modalities, from single unit recordings \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014} and neuronal population local field potentials (LFP) \cite{haider_millisecond_2016} to human electroencephalography (EEG) \cite{meisel_decline_2017}, magnetoencephalography (MEG) \cite{zhigalov_relationship_2015}, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, raut_organization_2020}. Neural timescales characterize the length of time in which an input is integrated and maintained by neuronal populations. That is, if an input causes a change in neural activity that then immediately reverts back to random, its timescale will be very short, or fast: you can't predict future values from the current state. However, if an input causes a lasting, predictable structure in activity, its timescale will be longer, or slower: given its current state, you can predict future values. This is interpreted as that activity having a longer ``memory'' of the input. This interpretation of neural timescales as the maintenance of information, or memory, in a neural time series is what makes timescales particularly attractive from a computational neuroscience perspective. However, this is also what makes accurate measurement of timescales so critical, especially given the rapid emergence of studies that examine the functional role of timescales in cognition \cite{cavanagh_diversity_2020, gao_neuronal_2020} and their alteration in disease states \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, wengler_distinct_2020}.

Related to the potential functional role of neural timescales is the fact that the structure of neural timescales is not random across the brain. Rather, they are spatially organized in a cortical hierarchy \cite{hasson_hierarchy_2008, kiebel_hierarchy_2008, murray_hierarchy_2014, gao_neuronal_2020, misic_brain_2010, raut_organization_2020}. The lowest levels of the hierarchy are associated with short timescales, on the order of milliseconds, in visual \cite{buracas_efficient_1998} and auditory \cite{yang_millisecond-scale_2008} sensory cortices. At higher levels along the cortical hierarchy, longer timescales, on the order of seconds, are associated with decision making \cite{brody_timing_2003} and working memory \cite{wang_probabilistic_2002} in the prefrontal and parietal cortices. This hierarchical distribution has also been observed across species, suggesting evolutionary preservation \cite{buzsaki_scaling_2013}. The current work focuses on neural timescales, rather than 2D and 3D spatial extensions, e.g. spatial scales. However, the spatial distribution of timescales means that a timescale should be estimated within a local region that is approximately stationary.

Returning to the temperature example, if we were to record temperature at much higher fidelity, we would see that temperature fluctuates periodically quickly, on a day/night basis, as well as more slowly, on a seasonal basis. These periodic oscillations introduce oscillations in the ACF. This is because an oscillation will be strongly anticorrelated with itself at every half-cycle lag and strongly correlated at every full-cycle lag. Just like with temperature, neural data also oscillates periodically, though often manifesting as more infrequent bursts. Neural oscillations are embedded within a non-oscillatory, aperiodic signal, which is thought to reflect asynchronous neuronal drive, capturing the timescale of the underlying neural activity \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. This mixing of aperiodic and periodic features means that the ACF also blends aperiodic timescales with neural oscillations. As we will explore in more detail below, this mixing of periodic and aperiodic processes can result in large biases and errors in estimates of the timescale from the ACF.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

A timescale is never measured directly. It is estimated as a parameter of a model, and in nearly every case that model is the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model: $T_{\text{tomorrow}} = \varphi T_{\text{today}} + \epsilon$. Assuming tomorrow's temperature will be very close to today's, the coefficient, $\varphi$, will approach one. As $\varphi$ approaches zero, today's temperature is increasingly less indicative of tomorrow's temperature and trends towards a Gaussian distributed random white noise process dominated by $\epsilon$.

Processes that are periodic and oscillatory are distinct from those that are aperiodic, such as modeled by an AR(1) process, but these oscillations must be taken into account when modeling temperature. Timescales are derived from the aperiodic component and are directly related to the AR(1) coefficient, but fail to capture or are biased by oscillations.

The AR(1) model may be applied to varying temporal scales. If $T_0$ is today's temperature and $T_k$ is the temperature in $k$-days, we can update the temperature model to: $T_k = \varphi^k T_0 + \epsilon$. This AR(1) form is a discrete-time analog of the stochastic motion of particles under Brownian motion, replacing temperature with velocity \cite{uhlenbeck_theory_1930}. In these lagged cases, the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$, depends on the discrete time step, $k$. This is equivalent to the value of the autocorrelation function (ACF) at the corresponding lag step, given the sampling rate, $f_s$. The ACF, $A(k)$, of an AR(1) process is described by an exponentially decaying function and may be alternatively expressed in terms of the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:acf}). The autocorrelation at lag one is equal to $\varphi$. The AR(1) model can simulate a signal, $x$, given a white noise input (eq. \ref{eq:ar}). The ACF and PSD are connected through the Fourier transform, described by the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, so the AR(1) model also has a form in the power spectral density (PSD): a Lorentzian, flat at low frequencies and falling as $1/f^2$ above a knee at $1/(2 \pi \tau)$ (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}).

\begin{align}
x_t &= \varphi\, x_{t-1} + \epsilon \label{eq:ar} \\
A(k) &= e^{\frac{-k}{\tau f_s}} = \varphi^k \label{eq:acf} \\
\tau &= \frac{-1}{f_s \log{\varphi}} \label{eq:tau} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \varphi\, e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:psd1}
\end{align}

Timescales, $\tau$, may be expressed as the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:tau}), and are bounded by the scale of the observation, defined by the sampling rate, $f_s$, and the length of the signal, $n$. Sampling rate lower bounds observable timescales, as very short timescales, relative to $f_s$, will be undetectable. For example, if a timescale related to temperature fluctuations is on the order of days, but temperature is sampled once a week, the daily timescale will be impossible to detect. Inversely, the length of the signal upper bounds detectable timescales. If a timescale is on the order of years, but only one week of data is collected, the yearly timescale will be undetectable.

Despite the increasing popularity of studying neural timescales, there are many caveats and limitations that impact the accuracy of fitting mathematical models to the data which, in turn, impacts the accuracy of timescale estimates and downstream interpretations. This paper evaluates and compares timescale estimation approaches via parameterization of the ACF and PSD, with respect to the corresponding AR(1) form (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}). The AR(1) model has forms in the signal, the ACF and the PSD, and each form is the basis of a common estimator: a lagged regression on the signal itself, an exponential fit to the ACF \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}, or a Lorentzian or spectral parameterization fit to the PSD \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, donoghue_parameterizing_2020}. Timescales, $\tau$, may be alternatively expressed in terms of the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$, equal to the correlation at lag one of the ACF, given an AR(1) process. The $\varphi$ parameter and the PSD of an AR(1) process have a long history \cite{yule_1927, gilman_power_1963} to build upon. The complexity of an AR(1) model increases as the order or number of past values, $p$, increases, e.g. an AR($p$) model. The extensive history of theoretical analysis, flexible complexity, and connection between $\varphi$ and $\tau$ make AR models a natural target when studying neural timescales.

Because ground truth timescales are unknown in neurobiology, we cannot know which method works under which conditions or why. To address this, neural signals are assumed to be described by AR(1) processes. Signals are simulated using a white noise input to an AR(1) system, or equivalently, a discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process \cite{uhlenbeck_theory_1930} or a branching process \cite{zierenberg_description_2020}. A binary spiking process, such as neuronal spike trains, may be simulated by convolving an exponentially decaying kernel with a Poisson point process \cite{meyer_inferring_2019}. This time series may be treated as the probability of a spike occurring and sampled to yield a binary process. The spikes may be binned \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}, giving a time series that has a desired timescale, dependent on the decay rate of the initial convolutional kernel. Regardless of simulation method, the core assumption is that the signal produced will be an AR(1) realization, and bound by: $0 < \varphi < 1$ or $0 < \tau < \infty$. These bounds narrow as the sampling rate or number of samples decreases.

\subsection{Bias}\label{sec:bias}

Simulations allow the study of timescale estimation bias when analytical solutions do not exist. Timescale estimation is evaluated when the AR(1) assumption holds and when it fails. Specifically, estimation bias is evaluated under: 1) model mismatch, when a process is better described by a higher-order AR($p$) model \cite{broersen_finite_2000}, 2) the presence of neural oscillations \cite{berger_uber_1929, buzsaki_neuronal_2004}, 3) finite duration or decreasing the number of samples in the signal \cite{broersen_finite_2000, schwarz_estimating_1978, zeraati_flexible_2022}, and 4) dynamic, non-constant timescales associated with behavioral or task states, or neural regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}. General recommendations for accounting for these sources of bias are provided.

To overcome the mixing of oscillatory and aperiodic signal features, we recently leveraged the mathematical relationship between the ACF and neural power spectral density (PSD) to show that (aperiodic) neuronal timescales can be more reliably estimated from the PSD, independent of the influence of oscillations. To do this, we used our power spectral parameterization approach for separating periodic and aperiodic processes, where oscillations manifest as narrowband ``bumps'' atop the aperiodic spectral component from which the neural timescale is derived \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}. Using this method, we showed that neural timescales in human participants are dynamic depending on task state, dilating or expanding during working memory \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}.

While the spectral approach for estimating timescales helps overcome some of the biases introduced by oscillations, short-time spectral estimates are noisy and very limited by their small sampling duration, especially within the context of a behavioral or cognitive task. This finite sampling bias, as it is known, also impacts ACF-based timescale estimates. These factors -- the presence of oscillations, the fact that timescales can themselves be dynamic over short timescales as a function of the behavioral state of the person or animal, the finite sampling bias, and the possibility that the signal is not first order at all -- all influence any given timescale estimate. That is, most common methods for quantifying neuronal timescales suffer from some kind of bias.

\subsubsection{Model Mismatch}

Model mismatch occurs when the AR(1) model is the incorrect model, after other biases have been carefully handled (\figpanel{fig:bias}{a}). This is a generalized extension of oscillatory bias, when there is temporal structure that does not neatly fall into the AR(1) form. In these cases, the AR(1) model is the incorrect model after oscillations have been accounted for, the signal is confirmed to be stationary, and the signal is sufficiently long, yet is not well described by an AR(1) model. When this occurs, the use of higher order AR($p$) offers a solution (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). Increasing the AR order flexibly models PSD and ACF, allowing for forms that deviate from the expected. AR($p$) models use multiple past values to predict the current (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). The AR($p$) model also has forms in the ACF and PSD (eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd}).

\begin{align}
    x_t &= \sum_{k=1}^p \varphi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon \label{eq:arp}\\
    P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{|1 - \sum\limits_{k=1}^p \varphi_{k} e\strut^{-j 2 \pi f k / f_s}|^{2}} \label{eq:arp_psd}
\end{align}

The denominator of eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} is a polynomial: $\varphi_1 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^1 + \varphi_2 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^2 + \dots + \varphi_p (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^p$. This allows application of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, which suggests that the AR($p$) model can fit any spectral form, e.g. universal approximation, given a sufficiently large order, $p$. However, it is desirable to keep $p$ small and the set of $[ \varphi_1, \varphi_2, \cdots, \varphi_p ]^{\top}$ sparse. Given universal approximation, the AR($p$) model may overfit other sources of variance, such as finite bias. Other potential sources of bias should therefore be evaluated while also minimizing the complexity of the AR($p$) model. One solution is to introduce sparsity through an L1 penalty on AR coefficients. Bayesian information criteria (BIC) may also be used to evaluate a set of models with varying $p$ to determine an optimal, minimized AR order \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978, vidaurre_survey_2013}.

Raising the order has a second cost, an interpretive one, and it is the one that has limited the use of AR($p$) models in this literature. A timescale is a single number, and an AR($p$) fit returns a list of $p$ coefficients that cannot be read as timescales. For $p > 1$, $\varphi_1$ is not an autocorrelation and need not even lie between zero and one, so the usual conversion in eq. \ref{eq:tau} does not apply. We show in the Results that this obstacle is removable, because an AR($p$) model can be read as a short list of decay times and rhythms.

\subsubsection{Oscillations}

In the context of neural data, timescales are typically estimated from the autocorrelation of LFPs or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. However both LFPs and single-unit spiking often exhibit rhythmic fluctuations -- oscillations. While an interesting signal in their own right, in the context of estimating neuronal timescales, oscillations can impact the accuracy of timescale estimation. Oscillatory activity manifests as a cosine in the ACF \cite{brunel_fast_1999} or as a narrow peak in the PSD \cite{he_scale-free_2014, buzsaki_scaling_2013}. The addition of a cosine can account for oscillatory components in the ACF model (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}). Optimizing the solution to cosine frequency is difficult and non-convex without strong priors, e.g., a priori knowledge of the oscillatory frequency. A poorly optimized cosine function, or lack thereof, will bias timescale estimates.

Recent methods allow for parameterization of oscillatory activity from the PSD \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}, modeled as Gaussian peaks. Either robust regression or explicit oscillatory modeling provides an alternative to ACF fitting. In the frequency domain, oscillations are limited to narrow frequency bands. Power outside of the oscillatory bandwidth is minimally affected, allowing for accurate recovery of the timescale. This is performed using the AR(1) form of the PSD (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}), or the approximate Lorentzian form \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. A mixture of oscillations and stochastic processes also arises from AR(2) models \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970}, e.g. with autoregressive coefficients: $\varphi = [1.95, -0.99]$. This produces a damped cosine in the ACF (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}). Such a rhythm is part of the dynamics rather than added to them, and the distinction between the two determines what can be done about it; the Results return to it.

Oscillations may also be removed from a signal. A narrow-band filter will, in addition to oscillatory power, greedily remove aperiodic power in the narrow-band. Singular spectrum analysis (SSA) is another option \cite{hassani_singular_2021}, a method based on eigendecomposition. SSA depends on combining principal components, or scaled eigenvectors, associated with the stochastic process, or by removing oscillatory components. These methods are optimal when oscillations are stationary or constant across the signal. The topic of bursting oscillations is similar to dynamic, non-stationary biases (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}), and depends on an accurate burst detection algorithm, such as applying thresholds to the analytic amplitude from the Hilbert transform \cite{feingold_bursts_2015}.

\subsubsection{Finite Sampling}

Stochastic processes suffer from a statistical bias related to finite duration recorded signals (\figpanel{fig:bias}{c}). Short duration time series increase estimation bias, limit the maximum detectable timescale, and decrease confidence. As the duration of a time series decreases and as $\varphi \to 1$, \textit{a)} timescale estimates are increasingly biased and \textit{b)} confidence intervals around the timescale estimate widen. Moving towards unbiased timescale estimation approaches is necessary for studying the fast temporal dynamics of task designs (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Dynamic timescales have been observed in working memory \cite{wasmuht_intrinsic_2018, gao_neuronal_2020}, decision making \cite{ganupuru_flexibility_2019} and auditory processing \cite{chien_constructing_2020} tasks.

In the context of event-related task designs or neuronal spiking activity, bias is often mitigated by averaging the ACFs of trials or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In these approaches, when there are multiple neurons, channels, or repeat trials, it is recommended to take the median or mean PSD or ACF across recordings, neurons, and/or time periods, prior to model fitting. Averaging across neuronal populations, regions, and time has been shown to reduce estimation bias attributed to short duration recordings \cite{spitzner_mr_2021}. However, averaging limits the ability to examine between-neuron, region, or trial timescale variability. This suggests that across regions or neurons, signals should be stationary, with a constant timescale, prior to computing an average ACF or PSD.

These methods are compatible regardless of how the ACF or PSD is computed. Parametric spectral estimators, such as AR($p$) models, are favored over non-parametric, Fourier-based methods, such as periodograms, as signals become shorter \cite{spyers-ashby_comparison_1998}. This is under the assumption that an observed process or signal aligns with the AR(1) model associated with timescales. The AR(1) model makes assumptions about neural data: as the length of a signal increases, the empirical ACF or PSD should approach and converge to the expected AR(1) forms (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}). How much data a given precision needs is worked out in the Results.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Timescales}

Dynamic timescales refer to a signal whose $\varphi$ or $\tau$ is non-constant across time, referred to as a violation of stationarity. This occurs when timescales are associated with behavioral or task states, or neural regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}. If a signal spans across these states or regions, a single timescale will combine distinct processes and ignore relevant temporal dynamics (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Timescales should be estimated from signals that are relatively homogeneous or stationary, with respect to a) anatomical or functional boundaries or b) behavioral or task states. This requires the selection of an optimal window size and accurate window clustering or a priori determination of which windows to combine. However, windowing increases finite sampling bias and each timescale estimate will be less confident. This leads to a min-max problem with the aim to minimize violations of stationarity while simultaneously maximizing window size.

In this paper, we illustrate how these common methods for estimating timescales -- from the ACF and the PSD -- are impacted by common neural signal qualities: model mismatch, oscillations, finite samples, and dynamic shifts in timescales. We show this in both simulated and real data, and provide recommendations for addressing these issues. Finally, at the risk of introducing yet another method with implicit and overlooked timescale estimation biases, we introduce higher-order autoregressive (AR) models as a viable alternative for quantifying neuronal timescales, and show how to read such a model as a small set of decay times and rhythms rather than as a list of coefficients.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We first test the estimators on processes whose timescale is known: an AR(1) process, an AR(2) oscillator, a higher-order AR($p$) process, and a scale-free process with no timescale at all (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{}, Supplementary Methods). Six estimators are compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in -- the signal, the ACF or the PSD -- and in the model order, AR(1) or AR($p$) with the order chosen by BIC, and they share every other step. \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} shows the two order-selected AR($p$) fits, to the autocorrelation and to the spectrum, on the three autoregressive cases and on one second of an AR(1); the AR(1) and time-domain estimators and the scale-free process are tabulated in the Supplement. When the process is AR(1), all six recover the constructed 50 ms timescale to within 2\%, order selection returns $p = 1$ in every domain, and the AR(1) model leaves under 1\% of the log spectrum unexplained. When the process is not AR(1), the same estimators disagree by up to a factor of 112, and every one of the disagreeing numbers is reproducible: repeated simulations return it to within a few percent. Reproducibility is not recoverability, and the two cannot be told apart without a check on the model.

\subsubsection{Model Mismatch}\label{sec:modes}

The response to a signal that is not first order is to raise the order, and the obstacle is that an AR($p$) fit returns coefficients rather than a timescale. The obstacle is removable, because the coefficients are not the natural description of the model.

Every AR($p$) model is equivalent to a small set of \emph{modes}, each one way the process rings down after an input: a decay with time constant $\tau$, and possibly a rhythm at frequency $f$ (\figpanel{fig:modes}{a}). The modes are the poles $\lambda_k$ of the model, the roots of its characteristic polynomial (Supplementary Methods). A real pole is a pure decay and a complex-conjugate pair is a damped oscillation, with
\begin{align}
\tau_k = \frac{-1/f_s}{\log|\lambda_k|}, \qquad f_k = \frac{f_s |\arg \lambda_k|}{2\pi}. \label{eq:mode}
\end{align}
For a real pole eq. \ref{eq:mode} reduces to eq. \ref{eq:tau}, so an AR(1) model is the special case of a single real pole. For a conjugate pair the mode's contribution to the autocorrelation is a damped cosine, $\rho_k(n) \propto e^{-n/(f_s \tau_k)} \cos(2\pi f_k n / f_s)$: $f_k$ sets the period and $\tau_k$ the decay of its envelope, and the two are independent, so a 10 Hz mode with $\tau = 150$ ms repeats every 100 ms and rings for 1.5 cycles before its envelope falls to $1/e$. An AR($p$) fit can therefore be reported as a short list of $(\tau, f)$ pairs rather than as $[\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_p]$. The number of modes lies between $p/2$ and $p$, because a real pole is one mode and a conjugate pair is also one mode.

The modes combine differently in the two domains, which is what makes a higher-order model hard to read by eye. In the ACF they add: the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of one exponential decay per real pole and one damped cosine per conjugate pair (\figpanel{fig:modes}{c}). In the PSD they multiply: the spectrum is a product of one factor per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{b}). Both decompositions are exact, and in our implementation both reproduce the model to better than one part in $10^7$. They are not the same decomposition and neither is the transform of the other (Supplementary Methods). The decomposition is the autoregressive case of a general result for linear time series \cite{west_time_1997}, previously used to track the components of a fitted autoregression through EEG seizures \cite{west_evaluation_1999}; the use we put it to is reading a fitted AR($p$) as a set of neural timescales.

Both kinds of mode have a $\tau$, and they mean different things. For a real pole, $\tau$ is the decay of the autocorrelation, as in AR(1). For a conjugate pair, $\tau$ is the decay of the envelope around the cosine: how fast the ringing dies away, not how long the process stays correlated. The product $f\tau$, the number of cycles a mode completes before its envelope decays by $1/e$, says whether it would be recognized as a rhythm. Below one cycle the mode rings down before completing a period and is a broad spectral shoulder rather than an oscillation. This is the common case in the recordings below, where the median in-band mode completes $0.67$ cycles and only $25\%$ exceed one. Importance is measured on the log spectrum and is not a share of the variance: the 40 Hz mode of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} ranks second in importance and carries $2 \times 10^{-5}$ of the variance, so it is invisible in \figpanel{fig:modes}{c}. A rhythm can dominate the log spectrum and be absent from the autocorrelation.

The constructed process of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} is chosen to be the case a single timescale cannot describe. It is an AR(7) with five modes: relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms, and rhythms at 10 Hz with a 150 ms envelope and at 40 Hz with a 60 ms envelope. The two slower relaxations are of comparable importance and an order of magnitude apart, so the process has no single relaxation time, and both rhythms ring for more than a cycle ($f\tau = 1.5$ and $2.4$), so each envelope decay is distinct from its period. Not every mode matters. Deleting a mode and refitting the overall scale degrades the fitted spectrum by an amount with a closed form (Supplementary Methods), and that degradation is the mode's importance. Ranked this way, four of the five modes reproduce the spectrum to $R^2 = 0.997$, and the 2 ms relaxation is the one left out. The ranking is a statement about the fitted spectrum, not a variance decomposition of the signal: the modes are driven by one shared input, so the process variance does not divide into per-mode shares.

The modes are recoverable from data in a way the coefficients are not. Across 200 independent 60-second realizations, the spectral AR($p$) fit recovered every mode, with median errors in $\tau$ of 8.0\%, 3.6\%, 2.5\%, 4.8\% and 1.9\% from the slowest mode to the fastest, and both rhythm frequencies to within 0.6\%. The first coefficient of the same fits was $5.92 \pm 0.01$; it exceeds one in every realization and has no timescale. An AR(1) fit to the same signals ran to the 20 s upper bound of its search in every realization, which is the fit reporting that no single relaxation time describes the spectrum. Reading the modes is the difference between an interpretable answer and no answer.

An oscillation is the most common form of mismatch, and how it fails depends on whether the rhythm is part of the dynamics or added to them (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{}, second column; Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is not autoregressive at any order: its spectrum contains a line that no product of poles reproduces, and its ACF contains a cosine that never decays. In the ACF this is fatal. The AR(1) fit falls from 41 ms to 10 ms as the rhythm grows from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, against a truth of 50 ms, and raising the order changes the failure without removing it: the AR($p$) ACF fit overestimates by 20 to 45\% (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). The higher-order bias is structural. An AR($p$) autocorrelation is a sum of one term per mode, but the weight of each term is set by the poles rather than being a free parameter, and the data are a mixture of an exponential and a cosine with a weight of their own. Reproducing the undamped cosine forces a pole onto the unit circle and the remaining poles move to compensate; given the exact population ACF, the answer still ranges from 17 to 57 ms with the order fitted. In the PSD the rhythm occupies a few bins, robust regression discards them, and both spectral fits stay within 3\% of the truth at every combination of rhythm frequency and power tested (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). An added rhythm can only be avoided, and the spectrum is where it can be avoided.

A stochastic oscillator is different. An AR(2) process with complex poles produces a rhythm that drifts in phase and amplitude, which is what neural rhythms look like \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970, feingold_bursts_2015}, and it is exactly an autoregressive process. Here raising the order works in both domains (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, second column): the AR($p$) spectral fit recovers the envelope decay to within 3\% and the AR($p$) ACF fit to within 9\% across a decade of decays and two octaves of frequency, while the AR(1) fits are wrong by factors between 0.016 and 133 that depend on both the decay and the frequency. A rhythm that is part of the dynamics should be modeled, and modeling it returns both its frequency and the decay of its envelope, which an AR(1) fit conflates with the aperiodic timescale. The solution to oscillatory bias is therefore to increase the AR order, to use robust regression in the spectral domain, or both, depending on which kind of rhythm the signal contains.

\subsubsection{Finite Samples}\label{sec:finite}

Computing confidence intervals around an estimated $\varphi$ has a long history \cite{mann_statistical_1943}. The least-squares estimate of the variance of the AR(1) coefficient is:

\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[ (\hat{\varphi} - \varphi)^2 ] &= \frac{1 - \varphi^2}{n-1} \label{eq:var}
\end{align}

However, this measure is increasingly biased as $\varphi \to 1$. Confidence intervals (CIs) may instead be computed as a weighted average between the least-squares and Cauchy estimators \cite{gallagher_small_2008}. This CI is useful for determining unbiased upper and lower confidence bounds of an estimated $\varphi$ or timescale (\figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b}). CIs are also useful for determining confidence in short window estimates, required when neural signals are non-stationary or when timescales are non-constant.

Propagating the bias and variance of $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau} gives a rule of thumb (Supplementary Methods). Both the bias and the precision of a timescale estimate depend on the recording only through the ratio of its duration $T$ to the timescale: the relative bias is about $-2\tau/T$ and the relative standard error about $\sqrt{2\tau/T}$. A bias under 10\% therefore needs about $20\tau$ of data and a standard error under 10\% about $200\tau$, whatever the sampling rate and whatever the value of $\tau$. For a 100 ms cortical timescale that is 2 and 20 seconds of stationary data per estimate, and precision improves only as the square root of duration, so halving the error costs four times the data. The sampling distribution of $\hat\tau$ is right-skewed (\figpanel{fig:finite}{c}), because eq. \ref{eq:tau} is convex in $\varphi$: a short recording more often underestimates the timescale, but errs further when it overestimates.

When there is no timescale to recover, a short window does something worse than add noise. On a scale-free process the ACF fit returns an estimate proportional to the length of the recording, over three decades of length, while the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat. A number obtained this way describes the analysis window, not the signal.

Using short time windows is what leads to this bias, and that is what makes non-stationarity the hardest of the four failures. The response to a timescale that changes with state is to shorten the window to the state, and a window short relative to the timescale is exactly the one that biases the estimate. Two responses are available. The first is to accept short windows and the bias they carry, correcting for it and averaging across trials where the design allows. The second, and the better one, is to design the experiment so that the state of interest lasts long enough -- tens to hundreds of timescales -- for a single window to be both stationary and precise.

\subsection{Empirical}\label{sec:realdata}

Simulations show what can go wrong; the recordings show how often it does. We applied one identical pipeline to eight publicly available recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity in humans, macaque, mouse and rat, with no dataset-specific artifact rejection, filtering or oscillation removal (Supplementary Methods). All software and analyses are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}, including robust PSD and ACF model fitting using a variety of model forms, including AR($p$). The aim is not a biological claim about any one recording but a count of how often the single-timescale model is adequate when the same question is put to signals of very different origin.

Timescales span species, recording modalities and brain regions, and the estimates reproduce the scale separation that motivates the hierarchy literature. Field-potential timescales were tens of milliseconds, between 11 and 52 ms by the AR(1) spectral fit; binned spike counts gave 232 ms; and precuneus BOLD gave 1.0 to 6.6 s. The three-order-of-magnitude gap between hemodynamic and electrophysiological estimates is expected, and it is a reminder that a comparison of timescales across modalities compares different models fit to differently filtered data \cite{raut_organization_2020}.

The recordings also often deviate from the AR(1) form, and the four biases of \figpanel{fig:bias}{} were usually present together. Signals were long enough to minimize finite bias, but every field-potential recording contained a narrowband peak, and the AR($p$) fit placed a rhythmic mode inside the expected band in six of the eight; the exceptions, resting-state fMRI and the spike counts, have no narrowband rhythm to find. Observed PSDs and ACFs (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,c}) contain narrow peaks in the PSD and damped oscillations in the ACF: in six of the eight the empirical ACF oscillates rather than decaying, and only the AR($p$) model follows it. The AR(1) model was insufficient, e.g. model mismatch bias, and the mismatch is measurable: an AR(1) model left between 5\% and 41\% of the log spectrum unexplained in the field potentials, 24\% in fMRI and 46\% in the spike counts, with a median of 14.5\% across the eight; an order-selected AR($p$) model reduced this to between 0.2\% and 3\% in the field potentials, 21\% in fMRI and 38\% in the spike counts, with a median of 1.4\%. The spike counts are the hardest case because a spike train adds a flat Poisson floor that the AR spectral model has no term for, and we fit them only where the process rises above that floor (Supplementary Methods).

Because no recording satisfied the AR(1) assumption, the estimators disagreed. Across the AR(1) and AR($p$) fits in the ACF and the PSD, the ratio of the largest reported timescale to the smallest ranged from 1.3 to 13 with a median of 6.1 (ratios of the median estimates of each recording); including the two time-domain fits it ranged from 1.3 to 179 with a median of 11.7. The largest disagreements were in the recordings with the strongest rhythms -- motor ECoG, posterior alpha and mouse LFP -- where the AR(1) fits report a fast decay and the AR($p$) fits a slower relaxation once the rhythm is described rather than absorbed. The most extreme single case is a lagged regression on sensorimotor MEG that returned 2.0 s against 11 ms from the ACF and 22 ms from the PSD, reading a sustained beta rhythm as persistence. The smallest disagreements, under 1.5-fold, were in resting-state fMRI and macaque ECoG, the two recordings whose spectra have the least structure to disagree about.

Thus higher order AR($p$) models, and identifying the dominant relaxation mode, are what the recordings call for. The modes say what each recording contains (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b,d,e}). Decomposing each spectral AR($p$) fit, and keeping only the modes whose timescale the analysis band can resolve (Supplementary Methods), leaves between one and ten modes per trace, seven or more in every field potential, of which the figure shows the three most important. Only resting-state fMRI is carried by a single component, a 2.6 s relaxation in every trace. Every other recording needs two or more, and the second is not a detail: in posterior alpha the largest component is a 113 ms rhythm, the second a 24 ms rhythm and the third a 46 ms relaxation; in rat hippocampus an 88 ms rhythm, a 46 ms rhythm and a 76 ms relaxation; in sensorimotor MEG a 26 ms beta rhythm, a 40 ms relaxation and an 18 ms rhythm; in macaque ECoG a 45 ms rhythm beside a 126 ms relaxation. In each of these a single reported $\tau$ is not a compromise between the numbers but a choice of one of them, made by the estimator. Identifying the dominant relaxation mode, and reporting the rhythm beside it, is what a higher-order model provides that a single timescale cannot.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Estimation bias prevents accurate quantification of timescales, limiting functional and physiological interpretations. Oscillatory and finite sampling bias may not exist separately and are often overlapping. Therefore, accounting for oscillatory activity is also important when studying, potentially dynamic, timescales, as oscillatory dynamics are associated with cognitive and disease states \cite{ward_synchronous_2003}.

Oscillatory, finite, dynamic and model-mismatch biases must therefore be accounted for jointly when studying timescales. The presence of oscillations suggests using the PSD to robustly fit the AR(1)-PSD form, or an AR($p$) form when the rhythm is part of the dynamics. Finite biases should be considered when designing tasks. Simulations and confidence intervals are helpful in determining the minimum number of samples required to quantify timescales or $\varphi$ within a desired degree of error, and the rule of thumb is roughly $20\tau$ of data for the bias to fall below 10\% and $200\tau$ for the standard error to do the same. Finite windows are required either by task design, or when evaluating if timescales are constant or dynamic. Even after accounting for oscillations, finite, and dynamic biases, empirical signals may not be of an AR(1) form, and a single $\tau$ may not be reflective of the entirety of a signal's dynamics. One solution is to increase the complexity of the model, e.g. AR($p$), and to read the result as modes.

What we have learned can be stated plainly. A neural timescale is a parameter of a model. When the model is the AR(1) process, as it is for an exponential fit to the ACF, a Lorentzian fit to the PSD, or a lagged regression on the signal, $\tau$ is the relaxation time of that process, and reporting it as a property of a region, a state or a subject is reasonable. When the model is wrong, $\tau$ is still well defined, still precise, still reproducible and still comparable across conditions; it is no longer the quantity the interpretation is about. Our simulations show that the two situations look identical from the outside, and our recordings show that neural data are usually in the second one. Three checks tell them apart at no cost: agreement between independent estimators, which is within 2\% when the data are AR(1) and up to 112-fold in simulation and 179-fold in the recordings when they are not; the fraction of the spectrum or ACF the fitted model leaves unexplained; and the order BIC selects. None of the eight recordings met that bar.

The four failures call for different responses. Model mismatch calls for a higher order, and the resulting model is readable as a list of decay times and rhythms, of which the dominant relaxation mode is the timescale an AR(1) fit was trying to report. An oscillation that is part of the dynamics is an AR(2) process and is best modeled, which recovers its frequency and its envelope decay; an oscillation added to the signal, such as a stimulus-driven steady-state response, is not autoregressive at any order and must be avoided, which is possible in the PSD and not in the ACF. Finite samples set a floor of about $200\tau$ per estimate, and non-stationarity pushes against that floor: a window short enough to be stationary is often too short to be precise, and the resolution is either to average short windows across trials or to design experiments whose states last long enough to be estimated. In real recordings cut into windows, the spread of estimates across windows was about an order of magnitude larger than finite sampling predicts (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}), so a confidence interval computed from the sampling formula understates the uncertainty of a windowed estimate, and one computed from the spread across windows does not.

\subsection{Recommendations}

We suggest the following when reporting a neural timescale. \textit{First}, state the model rather than only the estimator, for example ``the relaxation time of an AR(1) fit to the 1-100 Hz spectrum'' rather than ``the neural timescale''. \textit{Second}, report the ratio of recording length to estimated timescale, and treat estimates below roughly $200\tau$ as precision-limited. \textit{Third}, report the fraction of the spectrum or autocorrelation left unexplained by the fitted model. \textit{Fourth}, report at least one independent estimator; disagreement beyond about 1.5-fold is evidence of model mismatch rather than of noise. \textit{Fifth}, if the data contain a strong narrowband rhythm, prefer spectral-domain estimation and state whether the rhythm is being modeled or excluded. \textit{Sixth}, if an AR($p$) model is used, report the modes as $(\tau, f)$ pairs with their importances and the number needed to reproduce the fit, not the coefficient vector. \textit{Finally}, when windowing, take the confidence interval from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our simulations treat stationary processes, and non-stationarity is addressed only through windowing; it remains the hardest of the four biases because reducing it by shortening windows increases finite sampling bias. Seven of the eight recordings are continuous field potentials or a haemodynamic signal, and only one is a spike train, for which binning and Poisson variability add a flat floor that the AR spectral model cannot represent \cite{zeraati_flexible_2022, meyer_inferring_2019}; we handle it only by restricting the fit band, and a model with an explicit observation-noise term would treat it properly. The real-data analysis deliberately applies one pipeline to every recording, so the preprocessing a specialist would apply -- artifact rejection, burst detection, oscillation removal -- is absent by design, and the mismatch we report is an upper bound on what careful per-dataset treatment would leave. Finally, the mode decomposition is exact for the fitted model but inherits its assumptions. It is a parsimonious description of a spectrum, and whether a given mode corresponds to a separable biological process is a question the model cannot answer: summing two independent AR(1) processes yields a model whose fitted poles are pulled away from both original timescales, because the sum is not itself autoregressive.

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:smethods}

\subsubsection*{Conventions}

We write the AR($p$) model as in eq. \ref{eq:arp}, so that the characteristic polynomial is $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$ and stationarity requires all of its roots to lie inside the unit circle. Sign conventions differ between texts and the two common polynomials are reciprocals of one another; we use this one throughout, so that ``pole inside the unit circle'' means ``stationary''. The one-sided spectral density is
\begin{align}
S(f) = \frac{2\sigma^2/f_s}{\left|1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\varphi_k e^{-i 2\pi f k/f_s}\right|^{2}}, \qquad 0 \le f \le f_s/2,
\end{align}
normalized so that its integral is the process variance, matching Welch's method \cite{welch_use_1967}. For $p=1$ this is the discrete-time Lorentzian whose knee frequency is $1/(2\pi\tau)$. The theoretical autocovariance is obtained by solving the Yule-Walker system for the first $p+1$ lags and recursing thereafter.

\subsubsection*{Estimators}

Six estimators were compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in and in the model order, and they otherwise share everything: each consumes the same mean-centered signal and the same Welch power spectrum \cite{welch_use_1967}, so that differences between them cannot be attributed to preprocessing.

Making that sharing exact requires care with the autocorrelation. A spectral fit is restricted to an analysis band, 1-100 Hz for most of the electrophysiological recordings here, whereas the sample autocorrelation of the raw signal contains everything in the recording, including drift below the band. Fitting one estimator to the band and the other to the whole signal compares two answers to two different questions. We therefore compute the autocorrelation from the same band-limited spectrum the spectral estimators see, by inverse Fourier transform, with bins excluded as line noise filled by log-linear interpolation from their neighbours rather than zeroed, so that notching does not ring in the lag domain. The difference is not cosmetic: on rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 the raw autocorrelation gives an AR(1) timescale of 938 ms, set by slow drift the spectral fit never saw, against 14 ms once both estimators describe the same band. One further correction is needed. Welch's estimate is the average periodogram of tapered segments, so the autocovariance it implies is the true autocovariance multiplied by the taper's own autocorrelation, which for a Hann window has fallen to 0.66 at a lag one quarter of the segment length. Left in place this taper masquerades as a faster decay, so we divide it out and discard the lags at which the taper retains less than a fifth of its energy, where the correction amplifies noise faster than it removes bias. A model fit to the spectrum is carried into the lag domain by the same route, its spectrum evaluated on the same grid, restricted to the same band and inverse transformed, rather than by its analytic full-band autocorrelation, which would carry the model's extrapolation outside the band: below the lower edge sits most of the variance of a $1/f$-like model, and the two autocorrelations disagree there even where the fit is exact (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{c}). The components of \figpanel{fig:realdata}{d} are the partial fractions of the fitted spectrum, one term $A_j(1-\lambda_j^2)/(1 - 2\lambda_j\cos\omega + \lambda_j^2)$ per pole, transformed the same way; they add exactly to the model's band-limited autocorrelation, the lag-domain counterpart of the modes multiplying in the spectrum.

In the time domain, the AR(1) estimator is a conditional least-squares regression of $x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$, and the AR($p$) estimator uses Burg's method \cite{burg_maximum_1975}. In the autocorrelation domain, the model is $A\,\rho_p(k) + B$, where $\rho_p$ is the exact AR($p$) autocorrelation, $A$ absorbs a white-noise floor at lag zero and $B$ a constant offset; for $p=1$ this is the exponential-plus-offset model standard in this literature \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In the spectral domain, the model is eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} with a free scale, fit to $\log_{10}$ power, the convention of spectral parameterization methods \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}.

Robust (soft-L1) regression is used in the spectral domain only, and only when the signal contains a rhythm: in simulation, for the AR(2) oscillator and for the added sinusoid of the supplementary sweep; in real data, for every recording, since all of them contain a narrowband peak. It is offered in the spectral domain because a rhythm is an outlier there, occupying a handful of bins. In the lag domain a rhythm perturbs every lag, so down-weighting large residuals discards the fit rather than the rhythm, and robustness is not offered. Robustness is not free even in the spectrum: when there is no outlier to reject, down-weighting the tails of an ordinary residual distribution biases the fit, so it is a claim about the data and not a default. It is also a trade-off between the two things a fit can report. On an AR(1) background at $\tau = 50$ ms plus a broadband AR(2) rhythm at 10 Hz, which unlike a sinusoid the model can represent, the robust spectral fit recovered the aperiodic timescale at 53 ms over 20 realizations while discounting the rhythm; without robustness the fit found the rhythm in all 20 realizations at a median of 9.4 Hz and returned the aperiodic timescale at 68 ms. A study that wants both should report them from two fits and say so.

Both the autocorrelation and spectral fits are optimized over reflection coefficients rather than over the AR coefficients directly. Every point in the box $|k_i| < 1$ corresponds to a stationary model, the parameters are all of order one, and the fit cannot wander into a region where mode timescales are undefined; without this reparameterization 48\% of the spectral fits to real data returned non-stationary parameters. An alternative repair, useful when a fit is inherited from elsewhere, is to reflect any pole with $|\lambda| \ge 1$ to $1/\bar\lambda$ and divide the scale by $|\lambda|^2$; on the unit circle $|1 - \lambda z^{-1}|^2 = |\lambda|^2 |1 - z^{-1}/\bar\lambda|^2$, so this leaves the model spectrum unchanged while making the process stationary. Multiple starting points are used because the objective is non-convex when a rhythm is present.

Two further requirements are imposed on the fitted model, because the model is being used as an instrument for measuring a timescale rather than only as a description of the data. First, the model must contain a relaxation mode. Nothing in a least-squares fit requires this: a converged, well-fitting AR($p$) can place every pole in a conjugate pair, in which case it has no decay time to report. Before the constraint, a third of the fits to our real recordings came back with no relaxation mode, having described the data well. We therefore factor one real pole out of the characteristic polynomial, $A(z^{-1}) = (1 - a z^{-1})A_{\mathrm{rest}}(z^{-1})$ with $0 < a < 1$, and parameterize the remainder by reflection coefficients, so that a relaxation mode exists by construction, exactly as stationarity does. The requirement costs one order and is applied at every order, including two; on an AR(2) oscillator BIC then selects order three in the lag domain and four in the spectrum. Exempting order two was tried and abandoned, because it let a purely rhythmic AR(2) fit report the decay of its envelope as an aperiodic timescale. The constraint costs essentially nothing in fit: across our recordings the median change in each estimator's own objective was $+0.003$ for the lag-domain fit and $-0.000$ for the spectral fit. Second, the reported aperiodic timescale is the most important \emph{non-rhythmic} mode, where non-rhythmic means $f\tau < 1$, and a real pole qualifies trivially. A conjugate pair that rings for a third of a cycle is a damped relaxation, and reporting nothing for a model containing one would be an artifact of the algebra; and the guaranteed real pole is guaranteed to exist, not to matter. On mouse LFP the constrained spectral fit parks its required pole at 0.6 ms with negligible importance while the aperiodic structure sits in an 88 ms pair ringing 0.57 of a cycle. Importance, not pole type, decides.

Spectra are estimated by Welch's method, except for fMRI. A 296-sample record cannot afford Welch's trade: segmenting it leaves either too few frequency bins to fit or too few segments to average. We therefore use a multitaper estimate of the whole record \cite{thomson_spectrum_1982}, with time-bandwidth product $NW = 5$, which gives twice as many bins as the best Welch setting at a quarter of the bin-to-bin variability. Because adjacent multitaper bins inside the resolution bandwidth are not independent, the spectral fits use bins thinned to that spacing; leaving them in makes the record look eight times larger than it is, and order selection then buys orders it cannot support.

Models fit in either domain are scored in both. Spectral fit is the coefficient of determination of $\log_{10}$ power over the analysis band, with the overall scale refit; autocorrelation fit is the coefficient of determination over the fitted lags, with $A$ and $B$ refit. The two objectives are not equivalent, and the asymmetry between them is one of the results of the benchmark below.

\subsubsection*{Model order}

Order is selected by BIC \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978} in the domain the model is fit in: from the conditional Gaussian likelihood in the time domain, and from the residual sum of squares in the autocorrelation and spectral domains. The search runs over a log-spaced grid of orders up to 20. In the lag domain, neighbouring lags are strongly dependent, so the criterion uses an effective sample size, the number of lags divided by the lag at which the autocorrelation first falls below $1/e$, and stops early once it has passed its minimum. In the spectral domain the full grid is searched, because the grid is not uniform and the criterion is not unimodal over it. When the selected order reaches the top of the range this is recorded and reported, because it indicates a process the AR class cannot represent at any practical order rather than a well-determined order.

\subsubsection*{Modes}

The AR($p$) recursion in eq. \ref{eq:arp} has a characteristic polynomial $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, whose roots $\lambda_k$ we call the poles. Stationarity requires $|\lambda_k| < 1$. Dividing by $z^p$ gives
\begin{align}
1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} \varphi_k z^{-k} = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (1 - \lambda_k z^{-1}), \label{eq:factor}
\end{align}
so the spectral denominator in eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} factorizes into first-order terms and each pole contributes a decay time and, when complex, a frequency (eq. \ref{eq:mode}). Because the coefficients are real, complex poles come in conjugate pairs, and we combine each pair into a single damped oscillatory mode equivalent to a real AR(2) factor. A real pole is a pure relaxation mode. The term is borrowed from linear systems theory, where the modes of a linear system are the eigenvalues that govern how it relaxes.

Two points about this factorization are easy to state wrongly. It is a factorization of the transfer function, not a decomposition of the signal into independent AR(1) processes: the factors multiply in the spectrum, and an AR($p$) process is a cascade of first-order filters driven by one input. Conversely, the same poles do appear additively in the time domain. The impulse response is $\psi_n = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^n$, so the response to an input is a sum of decaying modes driven by a shared innovation, and the autocovariance decomposes the same way,
\begin{align}
\gamma(k) = \sum_j A_j \lambda_j^{\,k}. \label{eq:acfmodes}
\end{align}
Grouping the conjugate terms, the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of exponential decays and damped cosines, one per mode. The product form and the sum form are the factored and partial-fraction representations of the same rational function, and they are not transforms of each other: a product in the frequency domain is a convolution in the lag domain, not a sum, so a mode's additive term in eq. \ref{eq:acfmodes} is not the inverse transform of its multiplicative factor in the spectrum. What the partial-fraction form costs is separability: each residue $A_j$ depends on every pole, so a mode's contribution to the autocorrelation is not a property of that mode alone. This is also why a lag-domain AR($p$) fit to an added sinusoid is biased (main text; Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}): the residues are not free parameters, so the model has no parameter for the mixing weight of an external mixture. Because the modes share an input, the process variance does not decompose into per-mode shares.

\subsubsection*{Mode importance}

Writing $g_k(f)$ for the log of the $k$th pole factor, the model log spectrum is $\log \sigma^2 - \sum_k g_k(f)$: modes are additive in log power. Deleting mode $k$ and refitting the overall scale by least squares therefore leaves a residual whose root-mean-square in $\log_{10}$ power is exactly $\mathrm{sd}_f\{g_k\}/\ln 10$, and we use this as the mode's importance. An Itakura-Saito variant, equal to the asymptotic Whittle log-likelihood degradation \cite{whittle_estimation_1953, itakura_analysis_1968}, gives the same ranking in every case we examined. The error of a model retaining an arbitrary subset of modes is a quadratic form in the retained set, so the best subset of each size can be evaluated in closed form; the dominant modes are the smallest subset reproducing the fitted log spectrum with $R^2 \ge 0.99$.

For the real recordings, a mode is reported only if the analysis band can resolve its timescale. A mode slower than $1/(2\pi f_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $f_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the lower edge of the fitted band, has its knee below the band, so its timescale is an extrapolation of the model past the data rather than a measurement; left in, these are the near-unit-root poles that report a 393 s timescale from a 300 s recording, and they carry high importance precisely because deleting them changes the lowest frequencies. The criterion discards 9\% of the fitted spectral modes. Likewise a rhythmic mode counts as detected only if its frequency lies inside the fitted range.

\subsubsection*{Finite-sample theory}

For a mean-corrected least-squares AR(1) fit on $n$ samples, the classical results are $\mathbb{E}[\hat\varphi] - \varphi \approx -(1+3\varphi)/n$ \cite{marriott_bias_1954, kendall_note_1954} and eq. \ref{eq:var}. Propagating both through eq. \ref{eq:tau} to second order, and writing $T = n/f_s$ for the recording duration, gives in the regime $\tau f_s \gg 1$ that all neural recordings occupy
\begin{align}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] - \tau}{\tau} \approx -\frac{2\tau}{T}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}[\hat\tau]}{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{T}}. \label{eq:finite}
\end{align}
Both depend on the recording only through $T/\tau$, and both were verified against Monte Carlo. This is why estimation error is reported throughout as the ratio $\hat\tau/\tau$ rather than as $|\hat\tau - \tau|$ or $(\hat\tau - \tau)^2$: the ratio has the same distribution for a 10 ms and a 1 s timescale at the same $T/\tau$, and its logarithm is the natural distance for a scale parameter, whereas the absolute and squared errors scale with $\tau$ and $\tau^2$, and the squared error is set by the few large overestimates of the right tail. Confidence intervals are obtained by mapping the interval for $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau}, which preserves coverage because the map is monotone. The intervals of \figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b} are the least-squares intervals of eq. \ref{eq:var} evaluated at each estimate and averaged over simulations; the weighted least-squares/Cauchy construction \cite{gallagher_small_2008} refines them for $\varphi$ very close to one.

\subsubsection*{The integrated timescale}

As a model-free reference we use $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^\infty \rho(t)\,dt$ \cite{sokal_monte_1997, priestley_spectral_1981}, evaluated with the trapezoid convention so that a sampled exponential returns its continuous-time $\tau$ rather than $\tau + 1/(2f_s)$. Equivalently $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = S(0)/(4\gamma(0))$. It equals $\tau$ for an AR(1) process, is the variance-weighted mean of component timescales for a sum of independent AR(1) processes, and diverges for a long-memory process. It is not the envelope decay time of an oscillator: for a damped oscillator the anticorrelated troughs of the autocorrelation cancel most of the integral, which is why the AR(2) oscillator of the benchmark has $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = 3.3$ ms and an envelope decay of 150 ms. For an oscillatory process, how long activity stays correlated and how fast a perturbation decays are different questions with different answers.

\subsubsection*{Simulated processes}

Four processes were used in the benchmark, each with a closed-form spectrum and autocorrelation, simulated at 500 Hz for 100 s with 200 repetitions. Each isolates one way the AR(1) assumption can fail. \textit{AR(1)} with $\tau=50$ ms is the positive control: the model is correct, so an estimator that fails here fails for reasons of its own. \textit{AR(2) oscillator} has poles at $|\lambda| e^{\pm i\theta}$ with $|\lambda| = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}$ and $\theta = 2\pi f_0/f_s$, here $f_0 = 10$ Hz and $\tau = 150$ ms; it is a rhythm the model represents exactly, and its role is to separate a rhythm that must be avoided from one that should be modeled. The rhythm that must be avoided, \textit{AR(1) plus sinusoid}, adds $A\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)$ with random phase, parameterized by the ratio of oscillatory to aperiodic variance; the sinusoid is not an autoregressive process at any order, and it stands for the rhythm-contaminated recording. It is swept over frequency and power in Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{} rather than benchmarked at one setting. \textit{AR($p$)} is the AR(7) of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} and of the model-mismatch column of \figpanel{fig:bias}{}, the one higher-order process used throughout: relaxations of 200, 20 and 2 ms and rhythms at 10 Hz ringing for 150 ms and at 40 Hz for 60 ms. It is the only process inside the model family but above first order, which is what makes it necessary: every other failing process confounds model mismatch with order mismatch, and this one isolates the second. Its two slow relaxations are of comparable importance an order of magnitude apart, so that no single timescale describes it, and its rhythms ring for more than a cycle, so that the rule an estimator is asked to apply -- the most important non-rhythmic mode -- has an unambiguous target, the 200 ms relaxation, and no envelope decay can be mistaken for it. An alternative higher-order process, an AR(5) with a 250 ms relaxation, a 5 Hz mode ringing half a cycle and a 50 Hz rhythm ringing one cycle, is simulated alongside it and can be drawn in place of the AR(7) in every figure; it is not shown. \textit{Power law} is a long-memory ARFIMA process \cite{granger_introduction_1980, hosking_fractional_1981} simulated exactly by circulant embedding \cite{davies_tests_1987}; it is outside the family and has no characteristic timescale, so there is nothing for an estimator to recover.

Outside AR(1) the target is not given and has to be stated. We take it to be whatever the constructed process gives under the same rule the estimator is asked to apply, computed from the true poles: the relaxation time for AR(1), the envelope decay for the AR(2) oscillator, and for the AR(7) the most important non-rhythmic mode, which is its 200 ms relaxation. The rule and the construction can disagree, since a rhythm ringing less than a cycle is non-rhythmic by the rule, and defining the target by construction would then score estimators against a quantity they were never asked for; in the alternative AR(5) they do disagree, and its target is the 100 ms envelope of its 5 Hz mode rather than its 250 ms relaxation. Every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation was verified against long simulations.

\subsubsection*{Benchmark results}

When the process is AR(1), all six estimators recover the 50 ms timescale to within 2\% (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, first column). An added sinusoid is the sweep of Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}. For the AR(2) oscillator the three AR(1) estimators return 10, 138 and 250 ms for a 150 ms envelope decay, while all three AR($p$) fits recover it to within 2\% and the rhythm to within 0.02 Hz. For the AR(7) the three AR($p$) estimators return 183 to 195 ms against a target of 200 ms, while the three AR(1) estimators spread over a factor of 112: 179 ms for the autocorrelation fit, which the 200 ms relaxation happens to dominate, 1.57 s for the lagged regression, and the 20 s bound of its search for the spectral fit. The long-memory process produces estimates from 4 to 86 ms, a twenty-fold range, every one of them stable across repetitions.

Model mismatch tracks these failures. An AR(1) spectral fit leaves 0.8\% of the log spectrum unexplained for the AR(1) process, but 26\% for the AR(2) oscillator, 67\% for the AR(7) and 26\% for the power law; AR($p$) reduces these to between 0.03\% and 4.0\%. BIC returns $p=1$ for AR(1), $p=8$ for the AR(7), one above its true order, and runs to the top of the search range for the power law. Fitting each process's exact autocorrelation, which is what any consistent estimator converges to with infinite data, the mismatch collapses to zero at the correct order for the three autoregressive processes and only shrinks, without vanishing, for the power law, which no finite order removes.

The domain a model is fit in changes the model. Across the three processes that are not AR(1), fits to the spectrum describe the autocorrelation nearly as well as fits to the autocorrelation do, leaving between 0.2\% and 7\% of it unexplained (medians over repetitions). Fits to the autocorrelation describe the spectrum badly, leaving 14\% unexplained for the AR(2) oscillator, 11\% for the AR(7), and for the long-memory process more than the total spectral variance; on one realization of the long-memory process, the order-five model chosen in the lag domain has a spectrum spanning eleven orders of magnitude across the analysis band, where the data span two. Both domains use the same model, reparameterization, order selection and band, and the autocorrelation fit is the better of the two at describing the autocorrelation, so this is not an optimization artifact. A model estimated in the frequency domain is a usable description of the signal in either domain; a model estimated in the lag domain is not. On a scale-free process the lag-domain fit also returns an estimate proportional to the recording length, $\hat\tau \propto T$ over three decades, where the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and processing}

Eight publicly available recordings were used, selected to span species, recording modality and oscillatory regime rather than to address any single biological question. \textit{fMRI}: ABIDE resting-state BOLD \cite{di_martino_autism_2014}, six participants from the UM site, preprocessed by C-PAC without temporal filtering, TR 2 s. We take the precuneus, left and right, giving twelve traces. A single 296-sample BOLD run gives a spectrum too noisy to fit, and averaging over regions is the obvious remedy but the wrong one: regions differ in exactly the property being measured, and precuneus, medial prefrontal and occipital spectra have visibly different shapes in these data, so an average over all 111 Harvard-Oxford regions would describe no region. One default-mode hub keeps the average interpretable and makes a trace here the same kind of object as a channel elsewhere. The unfiltered pipeline is used deliberately, since the usual 0.01-0.1 Hz band-pass removes most of the spectrum a timescale is estimated from. \textit{MEG}: the FieldTrip cortico-muscular coherence recording \cite{schoffelen_neuronal_2005, oostenveld_fieldtrip_2011}, eight sensorimotor magnetometers during isometric contraction. \textit{EEG}: MPI-LEMON eyes-closed rest \cite{babayan_mind_2019}, posterior channels. \textit{ECoG}: pre-DBS primary motor cortex in Parkinson disease \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}; and macaque visual cortex during a grating task \cite{nagasaka_multidimensional_2011}. \textit{LFP}: mouse Neuropixels in VISam during natural movie viewing \cite{siegle_survey_2021}; and rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 \cite{mizuseki_theta_2009, mizuseki_hc3_2013}. \textit{Spiking}: spike counts of eight VISp units from the same Neuropixels session \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, binned at 50 ms over the same 300 s of natural movie. The neural-timescale literature rests largely on spike-count autocorrelations, so this is the one modality whose absence would be conspicuous.

Electrophysiological recordings were resampled to 250 Hz and fit over 1-100 Hz, with line-noise fundamentals and harmonics excluded from the spectral fits; the MEG is fit over 1-95 Hz because its spectrum carries a sharp peak at 100 Hz that is an artefact, not brain. Power spectral density was computed using Welch's method with 4 second windows and 50\% overlap. fMRI carries its own band, 0.01-0.24 Hz, because it is sampled three orders of magnitude more slowly. The spike counts are fit over 0.05-2 Hz rather than the 0.05-8 Hz their sampling rate allows: a spike train contributes a flat Poisson floor on top of whatever the membrane dynamics contribute, the AR spectral model $\sigma^2/|A|^2$ has no additive term for a floor, and above about 1.5 Hz the binned-count spectrum is within a factor of 1.5 of its own floor. Fitted over the full band the AR(1) model was fitting mostly shot noise, leaving 95\% of the log spectrum unexplained; restricting the fit to where the process is still above its shot noise improves it tenfold and brings the two spectral estimates from disagreeing by a factor of eleven to a factor of two. The criterion is that the process remain above its own shot noise, not a fixed frequency, and the band over which a spike-count model is fitted is a modelling choice that changes the answer and should be reported. No other filtering, artifact rejection or oscillation removal was applied.

\subsubsection*{Windowing real recordings}

Each recording was cut into non-overlapping windows of increasing length, from 0.5 s upward, and every window analysed with the identical pipeline (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Window length is measured in units of the timescale being estimated rather than in seconds, since a 1 s window is generous for a 4 ms timescale and hopeless for a 1 s one. Measured this way, the median estimate is close to window-independent: in six of the eight recordings it stayed between 0.73 and 1.9 times the estimate from the longest window, over more than three orders of magnitude of window length, the exceptions being macaque visual ECoG and the mouse spike counts, which sit at about a quarter of their final value until the window exceeds a thousand times the timescale. This is not what the same analysis showed before the autocorrelation was corrected. Taking the autocorrelation from the raw signal instead of from the analysis band, estimates collapse by up to two orders of magnitude at the shortest windows and climb steadily with window length, which reads as a window-length effect on the brain's timescales and is instead drift below the fit band entering the lag domain. An artifact of this shape is easy to produce by accident and hard to notice.

The spread between windows is a different matter. If finite sampling were the only source of variability, the spread of estimates across windows of a given length would follow the AR(1) sampling bound of eq. \ref{eq:finite}. At the longest windows available, the spectral estimate exceeded that bound in all eight recordings, by a median factor of 6 and by up to 30, and the autocorrelation estimate in all eight, by a median factor of 7 and by up to 29. This does not go away when the autocorrelation is corrected. At the durations where an estimate becomes precise enough to report, the dominant source of variability is change in the signal over time, and a timescale reported for a long recording is an average over a quantity that was changing throughout it.

\subsubsection*{Code}

All analyses use an open-source Python module with 200 unit tests covering, among other things, exact reconstruction of the AR($p$) spectrum and autocorrelation from the mode decomposition, agreement between the closed-form mode importance and a brute-force deletion computation, agreement of every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation with long simulations, the guaranteed relaxation mode, and agreement of eq. \ref{eq:finite} with Monte Carlo. Every figure is drawn by its own script in \texttt{revision/scripts/figures/} (\texttt{fig\_01.py} to \texttt{fig\_06.py} and the supplementary \texttt{fig\_s1.py}, \texttt{fig\_s2.py}), from the cached result tables, at the printed size with the printed point sizes; \texttt{plot\_figures.py} runs them in order. Code, figure scripts and result tables are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}.
