\abstract{
In attempt to understand the ``neural code'', we have developed conceptual and mathematical tools for interpreting neuronal activity across recording modalities. Neural activity is predictable from its own recent past, and that predictability fades with time. In sensory cortices, all forms of neural activity, from spiking to EEG, can reliably predict future neural activity up to a few hundred milliseconds later. In contrast, activity in association cortex predicts subsequent activity up to several seconds later. This temporal dependency of neural activity is often referred to as ``neural timescales''. There are several mathematical models for quantifying timescales: extracting a decay parameter from the autocorrelation function, fitting functions to power spectra, or directly from the time series with autoregressive models. Here, we show in both simulated and real neural data how common neural signal features -- model mismatch, neural oscillations, finite sampling, and dynamic timescales -- can yield inaccurate or uninterpretable timescale estimates. We show that higher-order autoregressive, AR($p$), models, designed to predict future data from $p$ past time points, handle these issues. The AR($p$) model decomposes into a set of modes or components. In autocorrelation, these components are represented as a sum of damped oscillators or relaxation functions, both with exponentially decaying structure. This method is applied to a variety of neural recordings from different specifies, spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Timescales}\label{sec:timescales}

Many real world processes are correlated at varying scales. For example, when trying to predict tomorrow's high temperature, a reasonable starting point is to use the high temperature from today. This also works to predict the high temperature the day after, and the day after that, and so on. One common approach for capturing these temporal relationships is through the use of an autocorrelation, which quantifies how correlated a signal is with itself at every temporal lag. The autocorrelation function (ACF) describes the resulting curve, which can be visualized as the correlation at each time lag (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}).

In natural processes, the ACF often decays exponentially, with correlation strongest at short time lags and decaying as the lag increases. The rate at which the ACF decays from correlated to uncorrelated is usually described as the ``timescale'' of the process. For purely random, Gaussian distributed, white noise processes, the decay is instant -- dropping from perfectly correlated at lag-zero to uncorrelated at all other lags. Timescales are quantified as the exponential decay rate of the ACF, where numerous real-world processes, ranging from the atomic scale with radioactive decay \cite{semkow_oscillations_2009}, to the astronomical with stellar flares \cite{anfinogentov_decaying_2013}, show this temporal dependence. Regardless of the domain or modality, the mathematical basis of timescales is the same, while interpretations and ground truth mechanisms will vary based on the signal's context.

Returning to the temperature example, temperature fluctuates periodically, on a day/night and seasonal basis. These periodic fluctuations introduce oscillations into the ACF. An oscillation is strongly anticorrelated with itself at every half-cycle lag and strongly correlated at every full-cycle lag. Weather does not generate a perfect sinusoids, so the amplitude and timing of each cycle will vary, and the correlation between cycles weakens as lag increases. The ACF of such a process is a damped oscillator: a cosine at the period of the cycle, multiplied by an exponentially decaying envelope. Two parameters describe this, a period and a decay rate, rather than the single decay rate of a purely aperiodic process. Yule described the sunspot cycle in these terms, as a damped oscillator, and introduced the autoregressive (AR) model to do so \cite{yule_1927}.

In this paper we compare the ACF, PSD, and AR models commonly used to estimate timescales, and show how each is biased when the assumed form is incorrect. This is shown in simulation, where the timescale is known, and in recorded data, where it is not, and recommendations are provided for addressing these biases. Imporantly, we show how to interpret complex, high-order AR($p$) models from the perspectively of timescales. AR($p$) has a corresponding form in all three domains and can be decomposed into a set of decaying and oscillatory components.

\subsection{Neural Timescales}\label{sec:neural}

In the context of neuroscience, timescales have been observed across recording modalities, from single unit recordings \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014} and neuronal population local field potentials (LFP) \cite{haider_millisecond_2016} to human electroencephalography (EEG) \cite{meisel_decline_2017}, magnetoencephalography (MEG) \cite{zhigalov_relationship_2015}, and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, raut_organization_2020}. Neural timescales characterize the length of time in which an input is integrated and maintained by neuronal populations. That is, if an input causes a change in neural activity that then immediately reverts back to random, its timescale will be very short, or fast: you can't predict future values from the current state. However, if an input causes a lasting, predictable structure in activity, its timescale will be longer, or slower: given its current state, you can predict future values. This is interpreted as that activity having a longer ``memory'' of the input. This interpretation of neural timescales as the maintenance of information, or memory, in a neural time series is what makes timescales particularly attractive from a computational neuroscience perspective. However, this is also what makes accurate measurement of timescales so critical, especially given the rapid emergence of studies that examine the functional role of timescales in cognition \cite{cavanagh_diversity_2020, gao_neuronal_2020} and their alteration in disease states \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, wengler_distinct_2020}.

Related to the potential functional role of neural timescales is the non-random structure of neural timescales across the brain. Rather, they are spatially organized in a cortical hierarchy \cite{hasson_hierarchy_2008, kiebel_hierarchy_2008, murray_hierarchy_2014, gao_neuronal_2020, misic_brain_2010, raut_organization_2020}. The lowest levels of the hierarchy are associated with short timescales, on the order of milliseconds, in visual \cite{buracas_efficient_1998} and auditory \cite{yang_millisecond-scale_2008} sensory cortices. At higher levels along the cortical hierarchy, longer timescales, on the order of seconds, are associated with decision making \cite{brody_timing_2003} and working memory \cite{wang_probabilistic_2002} in the prefrontal and parietal cortices. This hierarchical distribution has also been observed across species, suggesting evolutionary preservation \cite{buzsaki_scaling_2013}. The current work focuses on neural timescales, rather than 2D and 3D spatial extensions, e.g. spatial scales. However, the spatial distribution of timescales means that a timescale should be estimated within a local region that is approximately stationary.

Periodic signals introduce oscillations in the ACF. Neural oscillations are embedded within a non-oscillatory, aperiodic signal, which is thought to reflect asynchronous neuronal drive, capturing the timescale of the underlying neural activity \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. This mixing of aperiodic and periodic features means that the ACF also blends aperiodic timescales with neural oscillations. As we will explore in more detail below, this mixing of periodic and aperiodic processes can result in large biases and errors in estimates of the timescale from the ACF.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

Timescales are estimated as a parameter of a model, and is usually a form of the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model: $T_{\text{tomorrow}} = \varphi T_{\text{today}} + \epsilon$. Assuming tomorrow's temperature will be very close to today's, the coefficient, $\varphi$, will approach one. As $\varphi$ approaches zero, today's temperature is increasingly less indicative of tomorrow's temperature and trends towards a Gaussian distributed random white noise process dominated by $\epsilon$.

The AR(1) model may be applied to varying temporal scales. If $T_0$ is today's temperature and $T_k$ is the temperature in $k$-days, we can update the temperature model to: $T_k = \varphi^k T_0 + \epsilon$. In these lagged cases, the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$, depends on the discrete time step, $k$. This is equivalent to the value of the autocorrelation function (ACF) at the corresponding lag step, given the sampling rate, $f_s$. The ACF, $A(k)$, of an AR(1) process is described by an exponentially decaying function and may be alternatively expressed in terms of the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:acf}). The autocorrelation at lag one is equal to $\varphi$. The AR(1) model can simulate a signal, $x$, given a white noise input (eq. \ref{eq:ar}). The ACF and PSD are connected through the Fourier transform, described by the Wiener-Khinchin theorem, so the AR(1) model also has a form in the power spectral density (PSD) (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}).

Timescales, $\tau$, may be expressed as the AR(1) coefficient, $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:tau}), and are bounded by the scale of the observation, defined by the sampling rate, $f_s$, and the length of the signal, $n$. Sampling rate lower bounds observable timescales, as very short timescales, relative to $f_s$, will be undetectable. For example, if a timescale related to temperature fluctuations is on the order of days, but temperature is sampled once a week, the daily timescale will be impossible to detect. Inversely, the length of the signal upper bounds detectable timescales. If a timescale is on the order of years, but only one week of data is collected, the yearly timescale will be undetectable.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \varphi\, x_{t-1} + \epsilon \label{eq:ar} \\
A(k) &= e^{\frac{-k}{\tau f_s}} = \varphi^k \label{eq:acf} \\
\tau &= \frac{-1}{f_s \log{\varphi}} \label{eq:tau} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \varphi\, e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:psd1}
\end{align}

Neural processes often contain complex structure, beyond what an AR(1) model can sufficiently describe. A solution is increasing complexity through AR($p$) models that use the last $p$ values to predict the next, with one coefficient per lag (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). The ACF and PSD also have corresponding AR($p$) forms. Every past value the model uses adds a term to the ACF, so that the correlation at each lag is built from the correlations at the $p$ lags before it (eq. \ref{eq:arp_acf}), and adds a term to the PSD (eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd}). This lets both take shapes an AR(1) cannot, such as an ACF that dips below zero before returning, or a spectrum with both periodic and aperiodic components.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \sum_{k=1}^p \varphi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon \label{eq:arp} \\
A(k) &= \sum_{m=1}^p \varphi_m A(k-m), \qquad k > 0 \label{eq:arp_acf} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \sum\limits_{k=1}^p \varphi_{k} e\strut^{-j 2 \pi f k / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:arp_psd}
\end{align}

AR($p$) models lose the single parameter interpretion of the process: the timescales. With more than one lag, we must interpret a set of coefficients. Each one measures what its own lag adds beyond the others, so $\varphi_1$ is no longer a correlation and the ACF is no longer a single exponential. However, we will show that AR($p$) model can be factored into a set of processes that correspond to timescales and oscillators.

\subsection{Bias}\label{sec:bias}

Simulations allow the study of timescale estimation bias when analytical solutions do not exist. Timescale estimation is evaluated when the AR(1) assumption fails. Specifically, estimation bias is evaluated under: \textbf{1)} model mismatch, when a process is better described by a higher-order AR($p$) model \cite{broersen_finite_2000}, \textbf{2)} the presence of neural oscillations \cite{berger_uber_1929, buzsaki_neuronal_2004}, \textbf{3)} finite duration or decreasing the number of samples in the signal \cite{broersen_finite_2000, schwarz_estimating_1978, zeraati_flexible_2022}, and \textbf{4)} dynamic, non-stationary timescales associated with behavioral or task states, or neural regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}.

The present of oscillations is one type of model mismatch. In general, model mismatch is handeled by increasing the complexity of the model to AR($p$). Finite bias is a data collection problem must be addressed at the level of experimental design.

\subssection{Model Mismatch}

Model mismatch occurs when the AR(1) model is the incorrect model, after other biases have been carefully handled (\figpanel{fig:bias}{a}). This is a generalized extension of oscillatory bias, when there is temporal structure that does not neatly fall into the AR(1) form. In these cases, the AR(1) model is the incorrect model after oscillations have been accounted for, the signal is confirmed to be stationary, and the signal is sufficiently long, yet is not well described by an AR(1) model. When this occurs, the use of higher order AR($p$) offers a solution (eq. \ref{eq:arp}). Increasing the AR order flexibly models PSD and ACF, allowing for forms that deviate from the expected.

The denominator of eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} is a polynomial: $\varphi_1 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^1 + \varphi_2 (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^2 + \dots + \varphi_p (e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s})^p$. This allows application of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, suggesting the AR($p$) model can fit any spectral form, i.e. universal approximation, given a sufficiently large order, $p$. The AR($p$) model may overfit other sources of variance, such as finite bias, thus it is desirable to keep $p$ as small as possible. Bayesian information criteria (BIC) may also be used to evaluate a set of models with varying $p$ to determine an optimal, minimized AR order \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978, vidaurre_survey_2013}.

Raising the order has a interpretive cost and it has limited the use of AR($p$) models in this literature. A timescale is a single number, and an AR($p$) fit returns a vector of $p$ coefficients. We show in the results section, that AR($p$) model is interpretable as a set of decaying and damped oscillatory processes, each with timescale.

\subsection{Oscillations}

In the context of neural data, timescales are typically estimated from the autocorrelation of LFPs or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. However both LFPs and single-unit spiking often exhibit rhythmic fluctuations -- oscillations. Oscillatory activity manifests as a cosine in the ACF \cite{brunel_fast_1999} or as a narrow peak in the PSD \cite{he_scale-free_2014, buzsaki_scaling_2013}. An AR(1) model has no term for either, so the timescale it reports absorbs the rhythm and is biased (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}).

Higher order models capture oscillations. An AR(2) process is a damped oscillator \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970}: a cosine in the ACF under an exponentially decaying envelope, and a peak of finite width in the PSD. Each further pair of coefficients adds another such rhythm, so an AR($p$) model describes the oscillations and the aperiodic decay together, and each rhythm is reported as a frequency and a timescale, the decay of its envelope. Neural rhythms wax and wane, drift in frequency, and often appear as brief bursts \cite{feingold_bursts_2015}, which is what a damped oscillator describes and what the empirical neural recordings show (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,b}).

The exception is a perfectly sustained sinusoid, such as line noise or a steady-state response. Its ACF is a cosine that never decays and its PSD is a line at a single frequency, which no AR model produces at any order. Such a rhythm should be excluded rather than modeled. This is possible in the PSD, where it occupies a few bins and can be discarded by robust regression or modeled explicitly as a peak \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}, and not in the ACF, where it perturbs every lag.

\subsubsection{Finite Sampling}

Stochastic processes suffer from statistical bias related to finite duration recorded signals (\figpanel{fig:bias}{c}). Decreasing the duration of a time series increases timescale estimation bias, limits the maximum detectable timescale, and decreases confidence. Moving towards unbiased timescale estimation approaches is necessary for studying the windows temporal dynamics of task designs (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Dynamic timescales have been observed in working memory \cite{wasmuht_intrinsic_2018, gao_neuronal_2020}, decision making \cite{ganupuru_flexibility_2019} and auditory processing \cite{chien_constructing_2020} tasks.

In the context of event-related task designs or neuronal spiking activity, bias is often mitigated by averaging the ACFs of trials or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In these approaches, when there are multiple neurons, channels, or repeat trials, it is recommended to take the median or mean PSD or ACF across recordings, neurons, and/or time periods, prior to model fitting. Averaging across neuronal populations, regions, and time has been shown to reduce estimation bias attributed to short duration recordings \cite{spitzner_mr_2021}. However, averaging limits the ability to examine between-neuron, region, or trial timescale variability. This suggests that across regions or neurons, signals should be stationary and from similar distributions.

\subsubsection{Non-stationarity}

A signal is stationary when its statistics do not change over time, and non-stationary otherwise. In the terms used here, a dynamic signal is one whose AR($p$) parameters, and therefore its timescale, are not constant across the recording. This occurs when a signal spans varying cognitive or neural states. Fit to such a signal, a single model combines distinct processes and reports a timescale that belongs to none of them (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}). Timescales should therefore be estimated from signals that are homogeneous with respect to anatomical or functional boundaries and to behavioral or task states. This can be done by windowing the recording into periods in which the state is constant, which requires choosing a window size and knowing which windows to combine, or by designing the experiment so that the state is held constant throughout. Windowing carries a cost, since shorter segments increase finite sampling bias and each estimate is less confident, leaving a trade-off between minimizing violations of stationarity and maximizing window size.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We first tested the estimators on simulated processes with a known timescale: an AR(1) process, an AR(2) oscillator, and an AR(7) process (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{}). Each process was constructed with a 200 ms timescale so that the estimates could be compared on one axis. Six estimators were compared: AR(1) and AR($p$) models fit to the signal, the ACF, and the PSD, with the AR($p$) order selected by BIC. \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} shows the two AR($p$) fits, to the ACF and to the PSD, and includes a fourth column in which the AR(1) process was recorded for only twenty timescales. The AR(1) estimators, the time-domain estimators, and a scale-free process are reported in the Supplement.

When the process was AR(1), all six estimators recovered the 200 ms timescale to within 5\%, BIC selected $p = 1$, and the AR(1) model left under 1\% of the log spectrum unexplained. When the process was not AR(1), the AR(1) estimators disagreed with one another by up to a factor of 112. These disagreements were stable: repeated simulations returned the same estimates to within a few percent. Reproducibility alone therefore does not indicate that an estimate is correct.

\subsubsection{Model Mismatch}\label{sec:modes}

Raising the model order corrects model mismatch, but the AR($p$) coefficients are difficult to interpret. Instead, we decompose the model into \emph{modes} (\figpanel{fig:modes}{a}). Each mode is a decay with a time constant $\tau$, and each oscillatory mode also has a frequency $f$. The modes are the poles $\lambda_k$ of the model, the roots of its characteristic polynomial (Methods). A real pole is a relaxation mode and a complex-conjugate pair is an oscillatory mode, with
\begin{align}
\tau_k = \frac{-1/f_s}{\log|\lambda_k|}, \qquad f_k = \frac{f_s |\arg \lambda_k|}{2\pi}. \label{eq:mode}
\end{align}
For a real pole, eq. \ref{eq:mode} reduces to eq. \ref{eq:tau}, so the AR(1) model is an AR($p$) model with a single real pole. An oscillatory mode contributes a damped cosine to the ACF, $e^{-t/\tau_k}\cos(2\pi f_k t)$, where $f_k$ is the frequency and $\tau_k$ is the decay of the envelope. In the ACF, the modes add, one decay or damped cosine per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{c}). In the PSD, the modes multiply, one factor per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{b}). Both decompositions are exact (Methods). An AR($p$) model can therefore be reported as a list of $(\tau, f)$ pairs. Each mode is also assigned an importance, the amount by which the fitted spectrum degrades when that mode is removed (Methods). The AR(7) process in \figpanel{fig:modes}{} has five modes: relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms, and oscillations at 10 Hz and 40 Hz. The four most important modes reproduce its spectrum with $R^2 = 0.997$, and the 2 ms relaxation is negligible.

The modes were recovered accurately from simulated data. Across 200 simulations of 60 seconds, the AR($p$) spectral fit recovered all five modes of the AR(7) process, with median errors in $\tau$ between 1.9\% and 8.0\% and both oscillation frequencies to within 0.6\%. In contrast, the first AR coefficient of the same fits was $5.92 \pm 0.01$, which is not a correlation and does not correspond to a timescale. The AR(1) spectral fit to the same signals reached the 20 s upper bound of its search range in every simulation. In the benchmark, the three AR($p$) estimators returned 183 to 195 ms for the 200 ms relaxation, whereas the three AR(1) estimators returned 179 ms, 1.57 s and 20 s (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, third column). These results show that the modes, rather than the coefficients, are the interpretable parameters of an AR($p$) model.

\subsubsection{Oscillations}\label{sec:osc}

The effect of an oscillation depends on whether it is generated by the process or added to it. The AR(2) oscillator in \figpanel{fig:bias}{b} is generated by the process: it drifts in phase and amplitude \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970, feingold_bursts_2015}, and it is autoregressive. Raising the order corrected the bias in both domains (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, second column). The AR($p$) fits recovered the 200 ms envelope decay to within 1\% and the 10 Hz frequency to within 0.01 Hz. The AR(1) fits returned 10, 139 and 251 ms, depending on the domain. We repeated this comparison across envelope decays from 20 to 150 ms and frequencies from 5 to 40 Hz (Methods). The AR($p$) spectral fit remained within 3\% of the envelope decay and the AR($p$) ACF fit within 9\%, whereas the AR(1) estimates were off by factors between 0.016 and 133. An oscillation generated by the process is therefore best handled by modeling it, which also returns its frequency and envelope decay.

A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is not autoregressive at any order, because its spectrum contains a line and its ACF contains an undamped cosine. In the ACF, raising the order did not correct the bias (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). As the sinusoid's power increased from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, the AR(1) ACF estimate decreased from the true 50 ms to 3 ms, and the AR($p$) ACF estimate ranged from 0.5 to 12 times the truth. In the PSD, the sinusoid occupies a few frequency bins, and robust regression excluded them: both spectral fits remained within 3\% of the truth at every frequency and power. An added oscillation is therefore best handled by excluding it, which is possible in the PSD but not in the ACF.

\subsubsection{Finite Samples}\label{sec:finite}

Short recordings bias the timescale estimate and reduce its precision, and both effects depend on the ratio of the recording duration $T$ to the timescale $\tau$ (\figpanel{fig:finite}{}). Propagating the bias and variance of the AR(1) coefficient \cite{mann_statistical_1943, marriott_bias_1954} through eq. \ref{eq:tau} gives a relative bias of approximately $-2\tau/T$ and a relative standard error of approximately $\sqrt{2\tau/T}$ (Methods). A bias below 10\% therefore requires approximately $20\tau$ of data, and a standard error below 10\% requires approximately $200\tau$, independent of the sampling rate. For a 100 ms timescale, this corresponds to 2 and 20 seconds of stationary data. Precision improves with the square root of the duration, so halving the standard error requires four times the data. The sampling distribution of $\hat\tau$ is right-skewed because eq. \ref{eq:tau} is convex in $\varphi$: short recordings more often underestimate the timescale, but overestimate it by larger amounts (\figpanel{fig:finite}{c}). The AR($p$) estimators show the same effect. On four seconds of the 200 ms AR(1) process, the central 80\% of AR($p$) estimates spanned a factor of five (\figpanel{fig:benchmark}{d}, last column).

Finite sampling also constrains the analysis of non-stationary signals. A timescale that changes with task or state requires windows short enough to be stationary, and short windows are biased and imprecise. Averaging across trials reduces this cost. Where possible, experiments should be designed so that each state lasts tens to hundreds of timescales, so that a single window is both stationary and precise.

\subsection{Empirical}\label{sec:realdata}

We applied the same pipeline to eight publicly available recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity from humans, macaque, mouse and rat (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{}; Methods). Timescales spanned three orders of magnitude across modalities, consistent with prior reports of a cortical hierarchy of timescales. Field-potential timescales ranged from 10 to 48 ms by the AR(1) spectral fit, binned spike counts gave 198 ms, and precuneus BOLD gave 2.9 s, with a range of 1.0 to 9.3 s across participants.

No recording was well described by an AR(1) model. Every field-potential recording contained a narrowband peak in the PSD, and in six of the eight recordings the ACF oscillated rather than decaying (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a,c}). The AR(1) model left a median of 13\% of the log spectrum unexplained, between 5\% and 52\% across recordings. The AR($p$) model reduced this to a median of 1.5\%, between 0.2\% and 38\%. Because the AR(1) assumption was violated, the estimators disagreed. Across the AR(1) and AR($p$) fits to the ACF and PSD, the ratio of the largest to the smallest timescale ranged from 1.3 to 11 across recordings, with a median of 5.4. Including the time-domain estimators, the ratio ranged from 1.6 to 115, with a median of 11. The largest disagreements occurred in the recordings with the strongest oscillations, where the AR(1) estimators returned a fast decay and the AR($p$) estimators returned a slower relaxation.

The modes of the AR($p$) fits describe what each recording contains (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b,d,e}). After removing modes that the analysis band cannot resolve (Methods), each trace contained between one and eleven modes, and the figure shows the three most important. Resting-state fMRI was described by one relaxation mode of 3.4 s, and the spike counts by a 420 ms relaxation and a slower oscillatory mode near 1.5 Hz in half of the units. Every field-potential recording required seven or more modes to reproduce its spectrum. For example, posterior EEG contained a 61 ms mode at 10 Hz, a 33 ms mode at 22 Hz, and a 70 ms relaxation; rat hippocampus contained an 82 ms mode at 9 Hz, a 46 ms mode at 21 Hz, and a 71 ms relaxation; sensorimotor MEG contained a 26 ms mode at 15 Hz, a 39 ms relaxation, and a 19 ms mode at 27 Hz; and macaque ECoG contained a 34 ms mode at 15 Hz and a 126 ms relaxation. The modes were consistent across the traces of each recording. The 10th to 90th percentile range of the dominant mode's timescale across channels and participants spanned a factor of 1.8 to 8, with a median of 3.3 (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{e}), compared to disagreements of up to 115-fold between estimators on the same traces. These results show that the AR($p$) modes identify the dominant relaxation timescale of each recording and report the oscillations alongside it.

Finally, we cut each recording into windows to evaluate non-stationarity (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). The spread of estimates across windows exceeded the spread predicted by finite sampling in every recording, by a median factor of approximately 13. This spread includes variation between channels and participants as well as changes over time. A timescale estimated from a long recording is therefore an average over a quantity that varies within the recording.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

In this work, we compared six timescale estimators on simulated and recorded neural data, and we showed that higher-order AR($p$) models can be interpreted as a set of modes: relaxation timescales and damped oscillations. We demonstrated that AR(1) estimators are biased by model mismatch, oscillations, finite samples, and non-stationarity, and that these biases produce estimates that are reproducible but incorrect. We showed that the modes of an AR($p$) model are recovered accurately in simulation, that they describe eight neural recordings across six modalities and four species, and that they are consistent across channels and participants where the AR(1) estimators are not.

A neural timescale is a parameter of a model. For an exponential fit to the ACF, a Lorentzian fit to the PSD, or a lagged regression on the signal, that model is AR(1), and $\tau$ is its relaxation time. When the AR(1) model is incorrect, $\tau$ is still precise and reproducible, but it no longer measures the relaxation time of the signal. Our simulations show that these two cases cannot be distinguished from the estimate alone, and our recordings show that neural data are usually in the second case. Three checks distinguish them. First, independent estimators agree to within a few percent when the data are AR(1), and disagreed by up to 112-fold in simulation and 115-fold in the recordings when they were not. Second, the fraction of the spectrum or ACF left unexplained by the model is small when the model is correct. Third, BIC selects $p = 1$ when the data are AR(1). None of the eight recordings passed all three checks.

The four biases require different solutions. Model mismatch is corrected by raising the model order, and the modes of the resulting model are interpretable: the dominant relaxation mode is the timescale that an AR(1) fit approximates, and the oscillatory modes are reported alongside it with their own frequencies and decays (\figpanel{fig:modes}{}). In every field-potential recording, more than one mode was required (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{}). An oscillation generated by the process is one of these modes. An oscillation added to the signal, such as line noise or a steady-state response, is not autoregressive at any order and must be excluded, which is possible in the PSD but not in the ACF. Finite samples set a floor of approximately $200\tau$ per estimate, and non-stationarity conflicts with this floor, because a window short enough to be stationary is often too short to be precise. In our recordings, the spread across windows was approximately an order of magnitude larger than predicted by finite sampling. Confidence intervals should therefore be estimated from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula. In addition, these biases rarely occur alone. Every recording examined here contained an oscillation, had a finite duration, and varied over time, so all four biases must be addressed together.

\subsection{Recommendations}

We suggest the following when reporting a neural timescale. \textit{First}, state the model rather than only the estimator, for example ``the relaxation time of an AR(1) fit to the 1-100 Hz spectrum'' rather than ``the neural timescale''. \textit{Second}, report the ratio of recording length to estimated timescale, and treat estimates below approximately $200\tau$ as precision-limited. \textit{Third}, report the fraction of the spectrum or autocorrelation left unexplained by the fitted model. \textit{Fourth}, report at least one independent estimator. For recordings longer than $200\tau$, disagreement beyond approximately 1.5-fold indicates model mismatch rather than sampling error (Methods). \textit{Fifth}, if the data contain a strong narrowband oscillation, prefer spectral-domain estimation and state whether the oscillation is modeled or excluded. \textit{Sixth}, if an AR($p$) model is used, report the modes as $(\tau, f)$ pairs with their importances and the number of modes needed to reproduce the fit, rather than the coefficients. \textit{Finally}, when windowing, estimate confidence intervals from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula.

\subsection{Limitations}

One major limitation is that our simulations are stationary, and non-stationarity is addressed only through windowing. Non-stationarity remains the most difficult of the four biases, because shortening windows to reduce it increases finite sampling bias. There is also an imbalance across modalities. Seven of the eight recordings are field potentials or a hemodynamic signal, and only one is a spike train. Binning and Poisson variability add a flat noise floor to spike-count spectra that the AR spectral model cannot represent \cite{zeraati_flexible_2022, meyer_inferring_2019}. We addressed this only by restricting the fit band, and a model with an explicit observation-noise term would be more appropriate. In addition, the empirical analysis applied one pipeline to every recording without artifact rejection, burst detection, or oscillation removal. The reported mismatch is therefore an upper bound on what dataset-specific preprocessing would leave. The band-limited autocorrelation used here (Methods) also differs from the raw autocorrelation used in prior work, and timescales from the two are not directly comparable. In one recording, they differed by a factor of sixty. Finally, the mode decomposition is exact for the fitted model but inherits its assumptions. Whether a given mode corresponds to a separable biological process cannot be determined from the model. For example, the sum of two independent AR(1) processes is not autoregressive, and the fitted poles of such a sum are shifted away from both original timescales.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Estimators}

Six estimators were compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in and in the model order, and share all other steps. Each estimator uses the same mean-centered signal and the same Welch PSD \cite{welch_use_1967}, so that differences between estimators cannot be attributed to preprocessing. In the time domain, the AR(1) estimator is a least-squares regression of $x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$, and the AR($p$) estimator uses Burg's method \cite{burg_maximum_1975}. In the autocorrelation domain, the model is $A\,\rho_p(k) + B$, where $\rho_p$ is the exact AR($p$) autocorrelation, $A$ accounts for a white-noise floor at lag zero, and $B$ is a constant offset. For $p=1$, this is the exponential-plus-offset model used in prior work \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In the spectral domain, the model is eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} with a free scale, fit to $\log_{10}$ power, following spectral parameterization methods \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}. Fits in either domain are scored in both: spectral fit is the coefficient of determination of $\log_{10}$ power over the analysis band, with the overall scale refit, and autocorrelation fit is the coefficient of determination over the fitted lags, with $A$ and $B$ refit.

\subsection{Autocorrelation}

Spectral fits are restricted to an analysis band, 1-100 Hz for most of the electrophysiological recordings, whereas the raw sample autocorrelation contains the entire recording, including drift below the band. To fit both estimators to the same signal, the autocorrelation was computed from the same band-limited PSD by inverse Fourier transform. Bins excluded as line noise were filled by log-linear interpolation from neighboring bins rather than set to zero, so that notching does not introduce ringing in the lag domain. This correction is large. In rat hippocampal CA1/CA3, the raw autocorrelation gave an AR(1) timescale of 938 ms, determined by slow drift below the analysis band, compared to 14 ms from the band-limited autocorrelation. A second correction accounts for the Welch taper. Welch's method averages periodograms of tapered segments, so the implied autocovariance is the true autocovariance multiplied by the taper's autocorrelation, which for a Hann window decreases to 0.66 at a lag of one quarter of the segment length. We divided this taper out and discarded lags at which the taper retains less than a fifth of its energy. Models fit to the PSD were transformed to the lag domain by the same procedure: the model spectrum was evaluated on the same frequency grid, restricted to the same band, and inverse transformed (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{c}). The ACF components in \figpanel{fig:realdata}{d} are the partial fractions of the fitted spectrum, one per pole, transformed in the same way. They sum exactly to the model's band-limited autocorrelation.

\subsection{Robust Regression}

Robust (soft-L1) regression was used only in the spectral domain, and only when the signal contained an oscillation: in simulation, for the AR(2) oscillator and the added sinusoid; in the recordings, for every dataset. An oscillation occupies a small number of spectral bins, so it can be down-weighted as an outlier in the PSD. In the ACF, an oscillation affects every lag, so down-weighting large residuals removes the fit rather than the oscillation. Robust regression trades one quantity for another. On an AR(1) process with $\tau = 50$ ms plus a broadband AR(2) oscillation at 10 Hz, the robust spectral fit returned the aperiodic timescale at 53 ms across 20 simulations while down-weighting the oscillation. Without robust regression, the fit identified the oscillation at a median of 9.4 Hz and returned the aperiodic timescale at 68 ms. Studies that require both should report them from separate fits.

\subsection{Constraints}

Both the autocorrelation and spectral fits were optimized over reflection coefficients rather than AR coefficients. Every point in the box $|k_i| < 1$ corresponds to a stationary model, all parameters are of order one, and the fit cannot enter a region where the mode timescales are undefined. Without this reparameterization, 48\% of the spectral fits to the recordings returned non-stationary parameters. Multiple starting points were used because the objective is non-convex when an oscillation is present.

Two additional constraints were applied because the model is used to measure a timescale. First, the model must contain a relaxation mode. A converged AR($p$) fit can place every pole in a conjugate pair, in which case it has no relaxation timescale to report, and before this constraint one third of the fits to the recordings did so. We therefore factored one real pole out of the characteristic polynomial, $A(z^{-1}) = (1 - a z^{-1})A_{\mathrm{rest}}(z^{-1})$ with $0 < a < 1$, and parameterized the remainder by reflection coefficients. This constraint costs one order and is applied at every order, so BIC selects order three for an AR(2) oscillator. It changed the median value of each estimator's objective by $+0.003$ in the lag domain and $-0.000$ in the spectral domain across the recordings. Second, the reported aperiodic timescale is the most important non-rhythmic mode, where a non-rhythmic mode is one with $f\tau < 1$, that is, one that decays before completing one cycle. A real pole satisfies this by definition. The required real pole is not always the most important non-rhythmic mode. For example, in mouse LFP the spectral fit placed the required real pole at 0.6 ms with negligible importance, while the aperiodic structure was captured by an 88 ms conjugate pair with $f\tau = 0.57$. Importance determines which mode is reported.

\subsection{Model Order}

The order was selected by BIC \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978} in the domain of the fit: from the conditional Gaussian likelihood in the time domain, and from the residual sum of squares in the autocorrelation and spectral domains. The search covered a log-spaced grid of orders up to 20. In the lag domain, neighboring lags are strongly dependent, so BIC used an effective sample size, the number of lags divided by the lag at which the autocorrelation first falls below $1/e$. When the selected order reached the top of the grid, this was recorded and reported, because it indicates a process that AR models cannot represent at any practical order.

\subsection{Modes}

The AR($p$) recursion in eq. \ref{eq:arp} has the characteristic polynomial $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, whose roots $\lambda_k$ are the poles. Stationarity requires $|\lambda_k| < 1$. Dividing by $z^p$ gives
\begin{align}
1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} \varphi_k z^{-k} = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (1 - \lambda_k z^{-1}), \label{eq:factor}
\end{align}
so the spectral denominator in eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} factorizes into first-order terms, and each pole contributes a decay time and, when complex, a frequency (eq. \ref{eq:mode}). Because the coefficients are real, complex poles occur in conjugate pairs, and each pair is combined into a single oscillatory mode, equivalent to a real AR(2) factor. The term mode is taken from linear systems theory, where the modes of a system are the eigenvalues that govern how it relaxes. The decomposition is a general result for linear time series \cite{west_time_1997} and has been used to track the components of a fitted autoregression through EEG seizures \cite{west_evaluation_1999}.

The factorization applies to the transfer function and does not decompose the signal into independent AR(1) processes: the factors multiply in the spectrum, and an AR($p$) process is a cascade of first-order filters driven by a single input. The same poles appear additively in the time domain. The impulse response is $\psi_n = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^n$, and the autocovariance decomposes in the same way,
\begin{align}
\gamma(k) = \sum_j A_j \lambda_j^{\,k}, \label{eq:acfmodes}
\end{align}
so that, after grouping conjugate terms, the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of exponential decays and damped cosines, one per mode. The product form and the sum form are the factored and partial-fraction representations of the same rational function. Each residue $A_j$ depends on every pole, so the weight of a mode in the autocorrelation is not a free parameter. This is why a lag-domain AR($p$) fit to an added sinusoid is biased (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}), and why the process variance does not divide into per-mode shares. Both decompositions reproduce the model to within one part in $10^7$ in our implementation.

\subsection{Mode Importance}

Let $g_k(f)$ denote the log of the $k$th pole factor. The model log spectrum is $\log \sigma^2 - \sum_k g_k(f)$, so the modes are additive in log power. Removing mode $k$ and refitting the overall scale by least squares leaves a residual whose root-mean-square in $\log_{10}$ power is $\mathrm{sd}_f\{g_k\}/\ln 10$. We define this quantity as the importance of the mode. An Itakura-Saito variant, equal to the asymptotic Whittle log-likelihood degradation \cite{whittle_estimation_1953, itakura_analysis_1968}, produced the same ranking in every case we examined. Importance is measured on the log spectrum and is not a share of the variance. For example, the 40 Hz mode in \figpanel{fig:modes}{} ranks second in importance but accounts for $2 \times 10^{-5}$ of the variance. The error of a model retaining a subset of modes is a quadratic form in the retained set, so the best subset of each size was evaluated in closed form. The dominant modes are defined as the smallest subset that reproduces the fitted log spectrum with $R^2 \ge 0.99$. This count depends on the cutoff. At $R^2 \ge 0.95$, the field-potential recordings required 6 to 11 modes; at $0.99$, 7 to 11; and at $0.90$, 1 to 6. We report the strictest cutoff. The three modes shown in \figpanel{fig:realdata}{} are the most important under all three cutoffs.

For the recordings, a mode was reported only if the analysis band could resolve its timescale. A mode with a timescale longer than $1/(2\pi f_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $f_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the lower edge of the fit band, has its knee below the band, so its timescale is extrapolated beyond the data. These near-unit-root poles report timescales longer than the recording and have high importance, because removing them changes the lowest frequencies. This criterion removed 7\% of the fitted spectral modes. An oscillatory mode was counted as detected only if its frequency was inside the fit band.

\subsection{Simulations}

Four processes were used in the benchmark, each with a closed-form PSD and ACF, simulated at 500 Hz for 100 s with 200 repetitions. Each process with a timescale was constructed with the same timescale, 200 ms, so that the estimates could be compared on one axis. \textit{AR(1)} with $\tau = 200$ ms is the positive control. \textit{AR(2) oscillator} has poles at $|\lambda| e^{\pm i\theta}$ with $|\lambda| = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}$ and $\theta = 2\pi f_0/f_s$, with $f_0 = 10$ Hz and $\tau = 200$ ms, two cycles. \textit{AR($p$)} is the AR(7) process of \figpanel{fig:modes}{}, with relaxations of 200, 20 and 2 ms and oscillations at 10 Hz (150 ms envelope) and 40 Hz (60 ms envelope). It is the only process that is autoregressive but above first order, so it separates order mismatch from model mismatch. Its two slow relaxations have comparable importance and differ by an order of magnitude, and its oscillations persist for more than one cycle, so the most important non-rhythmic mode is the 200 ms relaxation. \textit{Power law} is a long-memory ARFIMA process \cite{granger_introduction_1980, hosking_fractional_1981} simulated by circulant embedding \cite{davies_tests_1987}. It has no characteristic timescale (Supplementary Results). The finite-sample column in \figpanel{fig:benchmark}{} is the AR(1) process simulated for four seconds, with the Welch window and the autocorrelation set to one quarter of the recording, as in the windowing analysis. An alternative higher-order process, an AR(5) with a 250 ms relaxation, a 5 Hz mode with $f\tau = 0.5$, and a 50 Hz oscillation, was simulated alongside and can be substituted for the AR(7) in every figure.

Two sweeps extend the benchmark. The damped-oscillator sweep varied the AR(2) oscillator over envelope decays of 20, 50 and 150 ms and frequencies of 5, 10, 20 and 40 Hz. The added-sinusoid sweep added $A\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)$ with random phase to an AR(1) process with $\tau = 50$ ms, over six frequencies from 4 to 40 Hz and six power ratios from one eighth to four (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). Both sweeps used 20 repetitions per condition. For processes that are not AR(1), the target timescale is defined by applying the same rule as the estimator to the true poles: the relaxation time for AR(1), the envelope decay for the AR(2) oscillator, and the most important non-rhythmic mode for the AR(7). All theoretical spectra and autocorrelations were verified against long simulations.

\subsection{Finite Samples}

For a mean-corrected least-squares AR(1) fit on $n$ samples, the bias is $\mathbb{E}[\hat\varphi] - \varphi \approx -(1+3\varphi)/n$ \cite{marriott_bias_1954, kendall_note_1954} and the variance is
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[ (\hat{\varphi} - \varphi)^2 ] &= \frac{1 - \varphi^2}{n-1}. \label{eq:var}
\end{align}
Propagating both through eq. \ref{eq:tau} to second order, with $T = n/f_s$ the recording duration and $\tau f_s \gg 1$, gives
\begin{align}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] - \tau}{\tau} \approx -\frac{2\tau}{T}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}[\hat\tau]}{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{T}}. \label{eq:finite}
\end{align}
Both quantities depend on the recording only through $T/\tau$, and both were verified against Monte Carlo simulations. Estimation error is therefore reported as the ratio $\hat\tau/\tau$, which has the same distribution for a 10 ms and a 1 s timescale at the same $T/\tau$, whereas absolute and squared errors scale with $\tau$ and $\tau^2$. Confidence intervals were computed by mapping the interval for $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau}, which preserves coverage because the mapping is monotonic. The intervals in \figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b} are the least-squares intervals of eq. \ref{eq:var}, evaluated at each estimate and averaged over simulations. The weighted least-squares/Cauchy construction \cite{gallagher_small_2008} improves these intervals when $\varphi$ is very close to one.

\subsection{Datasets}

Eight publicly available recordings were selected to span species, recording modality, and oscillatory regime. \textit{fMRI}: ABIDE resting-state BOLD \cite{di_martino_autism_2014}, twenty participants from the UM site, preprocessed with C-PAC without temporal filtering, TR 2 s, left and right precuneus. The unfiltered pipeline was used because the standard 0.01-0.1 Hz band-pass removes most of the spectrum used to estimate a timescale, and a single region was used rather than an average over regions, because regions differ in the property being measured. \textit{MEG}: the FieldTrip cortico-muscular coherence recording \cite{schoffelen_neuronal_2005, oostenveld_fieldtrip_2011}, the 24 sensorimotor magnetometers with the strongest beta oscillation, recorded during isometric contraction. \textit{EEG}: MPI-LEMON eyes-closed rest \cite{babayan_mind_2019}, four posterior channels from each of five participants. \textit{ECoG}: primary motor cortex from 23 participants with Parkinson disease before DBS \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}, and 24 channels from macaque visual cortex during a grating task \cite{nagasaka_multidimensional_2011}. \textit{LFP}: 24 channels of mouse Neuropixels recordings in VISam during natural movie viewing \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, and 24 channels of rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 \cite{mizuseki_theta_2009, mizuseki_hc3_2013}. \textit{Spiking}: spike counts from 24 VISp units in the same Neuropixels session \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, binned at 50 ms over the same 300 s of natural movie.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Electrophysiological recordings were resampled to 250 Hz and fit over 1-100 Hz, with line-noise fundamentals and harmonics excluded from the spectral fits. The MEG recording was fit over 1-95 Hz because of an artifactual peak at 100 Hz. PSDs were computed with Welch's method using 4 s windows and 50\% overlap. For fMRI, sampled every 2 s, the window was 128 s and the fit band was 0.01-0.24 Hz. For spike counts, binned at 50 ms, the window was 60 s and the fit band was 0.05-2 Hz rather than the 0.05-8 Hz available, because a spike train contributes a flat Poisson floor to the PSD that the AR spectral model cannot represent, and above approximately 1.5 Hz the binned-count spectrum is within a factor of 1.5 of that floor. When fit over the full band, the AR(1) model left 95\% of the spike-count log spectrum unexplained; the restricted band improved this tenfold. No other filtering, artifact rejection, or oscillation removal was applied. Halving or doubling the spectral window changed the median AR($p$) spectral timescale by less than 5\% in motor ECoG, mouse LFP and rat LFP, by 6-18\% in MEG, macaque ECoG, fMRI and the spike counts, and by approximately 50\% in EEG, which has the shortest traces at 60 s.

\subsection{Windowing}

Each recording was divided into non-overlapping windows of increasing duration, from 0.5 s upward, and each window was analyzed with the same pipeline, with the Welch window and the autocorrelation set to one quarter of the window duration (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Windows were pooled across the traces of a recording, so the spread across windows includes variation between channels and participants as well as over time. Window duration is expressed in units of the estimated timescale, because a 1 s window is long relative to a 4 ms timescale and short relative to a 1 s timescale. The spread of estimates across windows of a given duration was compared to the AR(1) sampling bound from eq. \ref{eq:finite} at that duration.

\subsection{Thresholds}

Three thresholds are used in the text. The $200\tau$ floor follows from eq. \ref{eq:finite}: at that duration, the relative standard error of one estimate is 10\%. The 1.5-fold threshold for disagreement between estimators follows from the same equation. Two independent estimates at $T = 200\tau$ each have a 10\% standard error, so their ratio exceeds 1.5 in less than 1\% of cases from sampling alone. In the AR(1) simulations at $T = 500\tau$, the AR($p$) fits to the ACF and PSD disagreed by more than 1.5-fold in 4.5\% of repetitions, and the AR(1) fits never did. Below $200\tau$, the threshold does not apply: at $T = 20\tau$, sampling alone caused the two AR($p$) fits to disagree by more than 1.5-fold in half of the repetitions. The $f\tau < 1$ criterion for a non-rhythmic mode is a definition: a mode that decays before completing one cycle is a damped relaxation rather than an oscillation.

\subsection{Code Availability}

All analyses use an open-source Python module with 200 unit tests. The tests verify exact reconstruction of the AR($p$) PSD and ACF from the mode decomposition, agreement between the closed-form mode importance and brute-force mode deletion, agreement between every theoretical PSD and ACF and long simulations, the relaxation-mode constraint, and agreement between eq. \ref{eq:finite} and Monte Carlo simulations. Each figure is generated by a dedicated script from cached result tables, and the simulations, tables, and figures are tracked as a single reproducible pipeline. Code, figure scripts, result tables, and the extracted traces are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}. All recordings are publicly available: ABIDE, MPI-LEMON, the MNE/FieldTrip CMC dataset, Cole et al. 2017, NeuroTycho, the Allen Brain Observatory, and CRCNS hc-3.

\subsection*{Supplementary Results}\label{sec:sresults}

\subsubsection*{Benchmark}

For the AR(2) oscillator, the three AR(1) estimators returned 10, 139 and 251 ms for the 200 ms envelope decay, whereas all three AR($p$) estimators recovered it to within 1.1\% and the frequency to within 0.01 Hz. Across the damped-oscillator sweep, the AR($p$) spectral fit remained within 3\% of the envelope decay and the AR($p$) ACF fit within 9\%, whereas the AR(1) estimates were off by factors between 0.016 and 133, depending on both the decay and the frequency. For the AR(7) process, the three AR($p$) estimators returned 183 to 195 ms for the 200 ms relaxation, whereas the three AR(1) estimators spanned a factor of 112: 179 ms from the ACF fit, 1.57 s from the lagged regression, and 20 s, the upper bound of the search range, from the spectral fit. The power-law process produced estimates from 4 to 86 ms, a twenty-fold range, each stable across repetitions. On this process, the lag-domain estimate was also proportional to the recording duration, $\hat\tau \propto T$ over three decades, whereas the same estimator on the AR(1) process was constant.

Model mismatch tracked these errors. The AR(1) spectral fit left 0.7\% of the log spectrum unexplained for the AR(1) process, 27\% for the AR(2) oscillator, 67\% for the AR(7), and 26\% for the power law. The AR($p$) fit reduced these to between 0.03\% and 4.0\%. BIC selected $p=1$ for AR(1), $p=3$ for the oscillator (its true order plus the required real pole), $p=8$ for the AR(7), and the top of the search range for the power law. When each process's exact autocorrelation was fit, the mismatch was zero at the correct order for the three autoregressive processes, and decreased without reaching zero for the power law.

The fitting domain also affected the model. Across the three non-AR(1) processes, spectral fits described the autocorrelation nearly as well as autocorrelation fits did, leaving 0.2\% to 7\% unexplained. Autocorrelation fits described the spectrum poorly, leaving 15\% unexplained for the oscillator, 11\% for the AR(7), and more than the total spectral variance for the power law. Both domains used the same model, reparameterization, order selection, and band, so the difference is not an optimization artifact. Models estimated in the spectral domain described the signal in both domains, whereas models estimated in the lag domain did not.

\subsubsection*{Added Sinusoids}

A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is not autoregressive at any order: its PSD contains a line and its ACF contains an undamped cosine (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). As the sinusoid's power increased from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, the AR(1) ACF estimate decreased from the true 50 ms to 3 ms. Raising the order did not correct this: the AR($p$) ACF estimate ranged from 0.5 to 12 times the truth across the same grid. The weight of each mode in an AR($p$) autocorrelation is determined by the poles, and no set of poles reproduces the mixing weight of an exponential and an undamped cosine. In the PSD, the sinusoid occupied a few bins, robust regression excluded them, and both spectral fits remained within 3\% of the truth at every frequency and power.

\subsubsection*{Windowing}

In units of the estimated timescale, the median AR($p$) spectral estimate converged to within 20\% of its longest-window value once windows exceeded 40 to 2300 timescales, depending on the recording, with a median of approximately 550. In the shortest windows, the median estimate was between 13\% and 82\% of the longest-window value. When the autocorrelation was computed from the raw signal rather than from the analysis band, estimates decreased by up to two orders of magnitude in the shortest windows and increased steadily with window duration. This pattern resembles a window-length dependence of neural timescales but is caused by drift below the fit band entering the lag domain. In the longest windows, every estimator exceeded the sampling bound of eq. \ref{eq:finite} in all eight recordings, by a median factor of 11 to 17 and by up to 44. The band-limited autocorrelation did not remove this excess.

\subsubsection*{Integrated Timescale}

As a model-free reference, we use $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^\infty \rho(t)\,dt$ \cite{sokal_monte_1997, priestley_spectral_1981}, evaluated with the trapezoid convention so that a sampled exponential returns its continuous-time $\tau$ rather than $\tau + 1/(2f_s)$. Equivalently, $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = S(0)/(4\gamma(0))$. It equals $\tau$ for an AR(1) process, is the variance-weighted mean of component timescales for a sum of independent AR(1) processes, and diverges for a long-memory process. It differs from the envelope decay of an oscillator, because the negative lobes of a damped cosine cancel most of the integral. For the AR(2) oscillator in the benchmark, $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = 2.5$ ms and the envelope decay is 200 ms. For an oscillatory process, the duration of correlation and the rate of decay after a perturbation are different quantities.

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:smethods}

\subsubsection*{Conventions}

The AR($p$) model is written as in eq. \ref{eq:arp}, so that the characteristic polynomial is $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$ and stationarity requires all roots to lie inside the unit circle. Sign conventions differ between texts, and the two common polynomials are reciprocals of one another. We use this convention throughout, so that a pole inside the unit circle corresponds to a stationary process. The one-sided PSD is
\begin{align}
S(f) = \frac{2\sigma^2/f_s}{\left|1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\varphi_k e^{-i 2\pi f k/f_s}\right|^{2}}, \qquad 0 \le f \le f_s/2,
\end{align}
normalized so that its integral equals the process variance, matching Welch's method. For $p=1$, this is the discrete-time Lorentzian with knee frequency $1/(2\pi\tau)$. The theoretical autocovariance was computed by solving the Yule-Walker equations for the first $p+1$ lags and recursing for later lags. A fit with a pole at $|\lambda| \ge 1$ can be made stationary by reflecting the pole to $1/\bar\lambda$ and dividing the scale by $|\lambda|^2$, which leaves the model spectrum unchanged.
