\abstract{
Neural activity predicts its own future. In sensory cortex, activity stays correlated with itself for a few hundred milliseconds; in association cortex, for several seconds. This temporal dependence is summarized as a neural timescale, and timescales have drawn attention because a longer timescale is read as a longer memory of past input. A timescale is never measured directly. It is a parameter of a model, usually the decay rate of an exponential fit to the autocorrelation or the knee of a Lorentzian fit to the power spectrum, and both are the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), model written in a different domain. Here we show, in simulation and in eight recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity in four species, how model mismatch, oscillations, finite recordings and non-stationarity bias the estimate. When the data are AR(1), every common estimator agrees to within a few percent. When they are not, the same estimators disagree by more than an order of magnitude while each remains reproducible. Higher-order AR($p$) models remove the mismatch, and an AR($p$) fit can be read as a short list of decay times and rhythms, its modes, rather than as a list of coefficients. In every recording examined the AR(1) model was inadequate, and six of the eight needed two or more modes. We give recommendations for reporting a timescale so that these assumptions are visible.
}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Timescales}\label{sec:timescales}

Many real-world processes are correlated with themselves over time. When predicting tomorrow's high temperature, a reasonable starting point is today's high temperature, and today's temperature also says something about the day after, and the day after that, with less confidence at each step. The autocorrelation function (ACF) captures this dependence as the correlation of a signal with itself at every temporal lag (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}).

At short lags the correlation is high, and it falls as the lag grows. The rate at which the ACF decays from correlated to uncorrelated is the timescale of the process. White noise has the fastest possible timescale, dropping from perfectly correlated at lag zero to uncorrelated at every other lag. Timescales are usually quantified as the exponential decay rate of the ACF, and processes with this form occur at every scale, from radioactive decay \cite{semkow_oscillations_2009} to stellar flares \cite{anfinogentov_decaying_2013}. The mathematics is the same in every domain; what differs is the mechanism behind the decay and what the decay is taken to mean.

\subsection{Neural Timescales}\label{sec:neural}

Neural timescales characterize how long an input is integrated and maintained by a neuronal population. If an input perturbs activity that then reverts immediately to random, the timescale is short: the current state does not predict future values. If an input leaves a lasting, predictable structure in activity, the timescale is long, and the activity is said to hold a memory of the input. This reading is what makes timescales attractive computationally, and it is also why accurate measurement matters, given the growth of studies on the role of timescales in cognition \cite{cavanagh_diversity_2020, gao_neuronal_2020} and their alteration in disease \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, wengler_distinct_2020}.

Timescales have been measured in every modality, from single-unit spiking \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014} and local field potentials (LFP) \cite{haider_millisecond_2016} to human EEG \cite{meisel_decline_2017}, MEG \cite{zhigalov_relationship_2015} and fMRI \cite{watanabe_atypical_2019, raut_organization_2020}, and they are organized rather than scattered across the brain. Sensory cortices have timescales of milliseconds \cite{buracas_efficient_1998, yang_millisecond-scale_2008}; prefrontal and parietal regions involved in decision making \cite{brody_timing_2003} and working memory \cite{wang_probabilistic_2002} have timescales of seconds. This cortical hierarchy \cite{hasson_hierarchy_2008, kiebel_hierarchy_2008, murray_hierarchy_2014, misic_brain_2010, gao_neuronal_2020, raut_organization_2020} is conserved across species \cite{buzsaki_scaling_2013}. Timescales also change with state, lengthening during working memory \cite{wasmuht_intrinsic_2018, gao_neuronal_2020}, decision making \cite{ganupuru_flexibility_2019} and auditory processing \cite{chien_constructing_2020}.

Neural activity also oscillates. The oscillations are embedded in an aperiodic background that is thought to reflect asynchronous neuronal drive and to carry the timescale of the underlying activity \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. The ACF blends the two, and separating them is one of the problems addressed below.

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

A timescale is never measured directly. It is estimated as the parameter of a model, and in nearly every case the model is the first-order autoregressive, AR(1), process. For temperature the AR(1) model is $T_{\text{tomorrow}} = \varphi T_{\text{today}} + \epsilon$. If tomorrow's temperature is close to today's, the coefficient $\varphi$ is close to one; as $\varphi$ approaches zero, today's temperature says less about tomorrow's and the process tends toward the white noise $\epsilon$. The AR(1) process is a discrete-time analog of Brownian motion \cite{uhlenbeck_theory_1930}, and its coefficient and spectrum have a long history \cite{yule_1927, gilman_power_1963}.

The AR(1) model has a form in the signal, in the ACF and in the power spectral density (PSD), and each form underlies a common estimator (\figpanel{fig:ar1}{}). In the signal, each sample is predicted from the one before (eq. \ref{eq:ar}), so $\varphi$ can be estimated by regressing the signal on its own past. In the ACF, the model is an exponential decay whose value at lag one is $\varphi$ (eq. \ref{eq:acf}); the timescale $\tau$ is its decay constant, in units of time given the sampling rate $f_s$ (eq. \ref{eq:tau}). In the PSD, which is the Fourier transform of the ACF, the model is a Lorentzian, flat at low frequencies and falling as $1/f^2$ above a knee at $1/(2\pi\tau)$ (eq. \ref{eq:psd1}). An exponential fit to the ACF \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}, a Lorentzian or spectral parameterization fit to the PSD \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, donoghue_parameterizing_2020}, and a lagged regression on the signal are three estimators of the same parameter of the same model, and when the data are AR(1) they agree.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \varphi\, x_{t-1} + \epsilon \label{eq:ar} \\
A(k) &= e^{-k/(\tau f_s)} = \varphi^k \label{eq:acf} \\
\tau &= \frac{-1}{f_s \log \varphi} \label{eq:tau} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{\left|1 - \varphi\, e^{-j 2 \pi f / f_s}\right|^2} \label{eq:psd1}
\end{align}

Observable timescales are bounded by the sampling rate and the length of the recording. A timescale much shorter than the sampling interval cannot be detected: daily temperature fluctuations are invisible when temperature is sampled once a week. A timescale much longer than the recording cannot be detected either: a yearly cycle is invisible in one week of data.

Because the true timescale of a neural recording is unknown, simulation is the only way to learn which estimator works under which conditions. A signal with a known timescale is simulated by driving an AR(1) system with white noise, equivalently a discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck \cite{uhlenbeck_theory_1930} or branching \cite{zierenberg_description_2020} process, and a spike train by convolving an exponential kernel with a Poisson process and binning the spikes \cite{meyer_inferring_2019, murray_hierarchy_2014}. In each case the signal is an AR(1) realization with $0 < \varphi < 1$, and the test is whether an estimator returns the $\tau$ that was put in.

\subsection{Bias}\label{sec:bias}

The AR(1) model is an assumption, and a timescale estimate inherits every way that assumption can fail. Four failures cover most neural recordings (\figpanel{fig:bias}{}): model mismatch, when the process is autoregressive but not first order \cite{broersen_finite_2000}; oscillations \cite{berger_uber_1929, buzsaki_neuronal_2004}; finite samples, when the recording is short relative to the timescale \cite{broersen_finite_2000, schwarz_estimating_1978, zeraati_flexible_2022}; and dynamic timescales that change with behavioral state or across regions \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}. Each is visible in the signal, the ACF and the PSD, and each calls for a different remedy.

\emph{Model mismatch.} The AR(1) model is wrong whenever a signal has temporal structure that a single exponential cannot describe (\figpanel{fig:bias}{a}). This is the general failure of which the others are instances, and it is the one that remains after the others are handled: the signal is stationary, long and free of added rhythms, and its ACF still does not decay as one exponential. A higher-order AR($p$) model predicts each sample from $p$ past values (eq. \ref{eq:arp}), describes such signals, and has its own form in the PSD (eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd}) and in the ACF.

\begin{align}
x_t &= \sum_{k=1}^p \varphi_k\, x_{t-k} + \epsilon \label{eq:arp} \\
P(f) &= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1 - \sum\limits_{k=1}^p \varphi_{k}\, e^{-j 2 \pi f k / f_s}\right|^{2}} \label{eq:arp_psd}
\end{align}

Raising the order has two costs. The first is statistical: a more flexible model can absorb noise, so the order should be as small as the data support, and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) selects it \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978, vidaurre_survey_2013}. The second is interpretive, and it is the one that has kept AR($p$) models out of the timescale literature. A timescale is one number, and an AR($p$) fit returns $p$ coefficients. For $p > 1$, $\varphi_1$ is no longer an autocorrelation and need not lie between zero and one, so eq. \ref{eq:tau} does not apply to it. The Results show that this obstacle is removable, because an AR($p$) model can be read as a short list of decay times and rhythms.

\emph{Oscillations.} Recorded at higher resolution, temperature fluctuates on a day-night cycle and on a seasonal one, and each cycle appears in the ACF as a cosine, because an oscillation is anticorrelated with itself at every half cycle and correlated at every full cycle. Neural signals oscillate in the same way, though more often in bursts, and an oscillation appears as a cosine in the ACF \cite{brunel_fast_1999} and as a narrow peak in the PSD \cite{he_scale-free_2014, buzsaki_scaling_2013} (\figpanel{fig:bias}{b}). Timescales are typically estimated from the ACF of LFPs or binned spike counts \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}, and there the rhythm is a problem: adding a cosine to the ACF model can absorb it, but the cosine frequency is hard to optimize without knowing it in advance, and a poorly fit cosine biases the timescale. In the PSD the rhythm occupies a narrow band and the rest of the spectrum is untouched, so the timescale can be recovered by parameterizing the peak explicitly \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020} or by robust regression that treats it as an outlier \cite{gao_neuronal_2020}. A rhythm can also be intrinsic to the dynamics rather than added to them: an AR(2) process with complex poles produces a damped cosine in the ACF \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970}, and such a rhythm is better modeled than removed. The distinction between an added rhythm and an intrinsic one determines what can be done about it, and the Results return to it.

\emph{Finite samples.} A short recording biases the timescale and widens its uncertainty (\figpanel{fig:bias}{c}). As the recording shortens, or as $\varphi$ approaches one, the estimate is pulled toward shorter timescales, its confidence interval widens, and the longest detectable timescale falls. In event-related designs and in spiking data the standard remedy is to average the ACFs or PSDs of many trials, neurons or channels before fitting \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014, spitzner_mr_2021}. Averaging buys the effective duration a single epoch lacks, at the cost of any between-trial or between-neuron variability, and it assumes that what is averaged shares a timescale. The size of the bias and of the interval follow from the sampling theory of AR(1) estimates \cite{mann_statistical_1943, gallagher_small_2008}, which the Supplement collects and the Results use to state how much data an estimate needs.

\emph{Dynamic timescales.} A timescale that changes over time violates stationarity, and a single estimate over a signal that spans behavioral states or brain regions averages distinct processes together (\figpanel{fig:bias}{d}) \cite{gao_neuronal_2020, spitmaan_multiple_2020}. Timescales should be estimated within windows that are homogeneous with respect to anatomy and to task state. Shortening the window to satisfy this reintroduces finite-sample bias, so the two failures trade off against each other: the window must be short enough to be stationary and long enough to be precise.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}

We first test the estimators on processes whose timescale is known: an AR(1) process, an AR(1) process with an added sinusoid, an AR(2) oscillator, a higher-order AR($p$) process, and a scale-free process with no timescale at all (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_bench}{}). Six estimators are compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in -- the signal, the ACF or the PSD -- and in the model order, AR(1) or AR($p$) with the order chosen by BIC, and they share every other step (Supplementary Methods). When the process is AR(1), all six recover the constructed 50 ms timescale to within 2\%, order selection returns $p = 1$ in every domain, and the AR(1) model leaves under 1\% of the log spectrum unexplained. When the process is not AR(1), the same estimators disagree by up to a factor of 46, and every one of the disagreeing numbers is reproducible: repeated simulations return it to within a few percent. Reproducibility is not recoverability, and the two cannot be told apart without a check on the model.

\subsubsection{Model mismatch}\label{sec:modes}

The response to a signal that is not first order is to raise the order, and the obstacle is that an AR($p$) fit returns coefficients rather than a timescale. The obstacle is removable, because the coefficients are not the natural description of the model.

Every AR($p$) model is equivalent to a small set of \emph{modes}, each one way the process rings down after an input: a decay with time constant $\tau$, and possibly a rhythm at frequency $f$ (\figpanel{fig:modes}{a}). The modes are the poles $\lambda_k$ of the model, the roots of its characteristic polynomial (Supplementary Methods). A real pole is a pure decay and a complex-conjugate pair is a damped oscillation, with
\begin{align}
\tau_k = \frac{-1/f_s}{\log|\lambda_k|}, \qquad f_k = \frac{f_s |\arg \lambda_k|}{2\pi}. \label{eq:mode}
\end{align}
For a real pole eq. \ref{eq:mode} reduces to eq. \ref{eq:tau}, so an AR(1) model is the special case of a single real pole. An AR($p$) fit can therefore be reported as a short list of $(\tau, f)$ pairs rather than as $[\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_p]$. The number of modes lies between $p/2$ and $p$, because a real pole is one mode and a conjugate pair is also one mode.

The modes combine differently in the two domains, which is what makes a higher-order model hard to read by eye. In the ACF they add: the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of one exponential decay per real pole and one damped cosine per conjugate pair (\figpanel{fig:modes}{c}). In the PSD they multiply: the spectrum is a product of one factor per mode (\figpanel{fig:modes}{b}). Both decompositions are exact, and in our implementation both reproduce the model to better than one part in $10^7$. They are not the same decomposition and neither is the transform of the other (Supplementary Methods). The decomposition is the autoregressive case of a general result for linear time series \cite{west_time_1997}, previously used to track the components of a fitted autoregression through EEG seizures \cite{west_evaluation_1999}; the use we put it to is reading a fitted AR($p$) as a set of neural timescales.

Both kinds of mode have a $\tau$, and they mean different things. For a real pole, $\tau$ is the decay of the autocorrelation, as in AR(1). For a conjugate pair, $\tau$ is the decay of the envelope around the cosine: how fast the ringing dies away, not how long the process stays correlated. The product $f\tau$, the number of cycles a mode completes before its envelope decays by $1/e$, says whether it would be recognized as a rhythm. Below one cycle the mode rings down before completing a period and is a broad spectral shoulder rather than an oscillation. This is the common case in the recordings below, where the median in-band mode completes $0.67$ cycles and only $25\%$ exceed one.

The constructed process of \figpanel{fig:modes}{} is chosen to be the case a single timescale cannot describe. It is an AR(7) with five modes: relaxations at 200, 20 and 2 ms, and rhythms at 10 Hz with a 150 ms envelope and at 40 Hz with a 60 ms envelope. The two slower relaxations are of comparable importance and an order of magnitude apart, so the process has no single relaxation time, and both rhythms ring for more than a cycle ($f\tau = 1.5$ and $2.4$), so each envelope decay is distinct from its period. Not every mode matters. Deleting a mode and refitting the overall scale degrades the fitted spectrum by an amount with a closed form (Supplementary Methods), and that degradation is the mode's importance. Ranked this way, four of the five modes reproduce the spectrum to $R^2 = 0.997$, and the 2 ms relaxation is the one left out (\figpanel{fig:modes}{d}). The ranking is a statement about the fitted spectrum, not a variance decomposition of the signal: the modes are driven by one shared input, so the process variance does not divide into per-mode shares.

The modes are recoverable from data in a way the coefficients are not (\figpanel{fig:modes}{e}). Across 200 independent 60-second realizations, the spectral AR($p$) fit recovered every mode, with median errors in $\tau$ of 8.0\%, 3.6\%, 2.5\%, 4.8\% and 1.9\% from the slowest mode to the fastest, and both rhythm frequencies to within 0.6\%. The first coefficient of the same fits was $5.92 \pm 0.01$; it exceeds one in every realization and has no timescale. An AR(1) fit to the same signals ran to the 20 s upper bound of its search in every realization, which is the fit reporting that no single relaxation time describes the spectrum. Reading the modes is the difference between an interpretable answer and no answer.

An oscillation is the most common form of mismatch, and how it fails depends on whether the rhythm is part of the dynamics or added to them (\figpanel{fig:osc}{}). A sinusoid added to an AR(1) process is not autoregressive at any order: its spectrum contains a line that no product of poles reproduces, and its ACF contains a cosine that never decays. In the ACF this is fatal. The AR(1) fit falls from 41 ms to 10 ms as the rhythm grows from one eighth to four times the aperiodic power, against a truth of 50 ms, and raising the order changes the failure without removing it: the AR($p$) ACF fit overestimates by 20 to 45\% (\figpanel{fig:osc}{c}). The higher-order bias is structural. An AR($p$) autocorrelation is a sum of one term per mode, but the weight of each term is set by the poles rather than being a free parameter, and the data are a mixture of an exponential and a cosine with a weight of their own. Reproducing the undamped cosine forces a pole onto the unit circle and the remaining poles move to compensate; given the exact population ACF, the answer still ranges from 17 to 57 ms with the order fitted. In the PSD the rhythm occupies a few bins, robust regression discards them, and both spectral fits stay within 3\% of the truth at every combination of rhythm frequency and power tested (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_grid}{}). An added rhythm can only be avoided, and the spectrum is where it can be avoided.

A stochastic oscillator is different. An AR(2) process with complex poles produces a rhythm that drifts in phase and amplitude, which is what neural rhythms look like \cite{stralrowski_charts_1970, feingold_bursts_2015}, and it is exactly an autoregressive process. Here raising the order works in both domains (\figpanel{fig:osc}{f}): the AR($p$) spectral fit recovers the envelope decay to within 3\% and the AR($p$) ACF fit to within 9\% across a decade of decays and two octaves of frequency, while the AR(1) fits are wrong by factors between 0.016 and 133 that depend on both the decay and the frequency. A rhythm that is part of the dynamics should be modeled, and modeling it returns both its frequency and the decay of its envelope, which an AR(1) fit conflates with the aperiodic timescale.

\subsubsection{Finite samples}\label{sec:finite}

The finite-sample bias of an AR(1) timescale follows from the classical sampling theory of $\hat\varphi$ (Supplementary Methods), and it depends on the recording only through the ratio of its duration $T$ to the timescale: the relative bias is about $-2\tau/T$ and the relative standard error about $\sqrt{2\tau/T}$. A bias under 10\% therefore needs about $20\tau$ of data and a standard error under 10\% about $200\tau$, whatever the sampling rate and whatever the value of $\tau$. For a 100 ms cortical timescale that is 2 and 20 seconds of stationary data per estimate, and precision improves only as the square root of duration, so halving the error costs four times the data. The confidence interval around $\hat\varphi$ narrows accordingly from 100 to 1000 samples and widens as $\varphi$ approaches one, which is where neural timescales lie (\figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b}). The sampling distribution of $\hat\tau$ is right-skewed (\figpanel{fig:finite}{c}), because eq. \ref{eq:tau} is convex in $\varphi$: a short recording more often underestimates the timescale, but errs further when it overestimates.

When there is no timescale to recover, a short window does something worse than add noise. On a scale-free process the ACF fit returns an estimate proportional to the length of the recording, over three decades of length, while the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat. A number obtained this way describes the analysis window, not the signal.

This arithmetic is what makes non-stationarity the hardest of the four failures. The response to a timescale that changes with state is to shorten the window to the state, and a window short relative to the timescale is exactly the one that biases the estimate. Two responses are available. The first is to accept short windows and the bias they carry, correcting for it and averaging across trials where the design allows. The second, and the better one, is to design the experiment so that the state of interest lasts long enough -- tens to hundreds of timescales -- for a single window to be both stationary and precise.

\subsection{Empirical}\label{sec:realdata}

Simulations show what can go wrong; the recordings show how often it does. We applied one identical pipeline to eight publicly available recordings spanning fMRI, MEG, EEG, ECoG, LFP and spiking activity in humans, macaque, mouse and rat, with no dataset-specific artifact rejection, filtering or oscillation removal (Supplementary Methods). The aim is not a biological claim about any one recording but a count of how often the single-timescale model is adequate when the same question is put to signals of very different origin.

The estimates reproduce the scale separation that motivates the hierarchy literature. Field-potential timescales were tens of milliseconds, between 11 and 52 ms by the AR(1) spectral fit; binned spike counts gave 232 ms; and precuneus BOLD gave 1.0 to 6.6 s. The three-order-of-magnitude gap between hemodynamic and electrophysiological estimates is expected, and it is a reminder that a comparison of timescales across modalities compares different models fit to differently filtered data \cite{raut_organization_2020}.

Every recording deviated from the AR(1) form, and the four failures of \figpanel{fig:bias}{} were usually present together. Every field-potential recording contained a narrowband peak, and the AR($p$) fit placed a rhythmic mode inside the expected band in six of the eight; the exceptions, resting-state fMRI and the spike counts, have no narrowband rhythm to find. In six of the eight the empirical ACF oscillates rather than decaying, and only the AR($p$) model follows it (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b}). The mismatch is measurable: an AR(1) model left between 5\% and 41\% of the log spectrum unexplained in the field potentials, 24\% in fMRI and 46\% in the spike counts, with a median of 14\% across the eight; an order-selected AR($p$) model reduced this to between 0.2\% and 3\% in the field potentials, 21\% in fMRI and 38\% in the spike counts, with a median of 1.3\% (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{a}). The spike counts are the hardest case because a spike train adds a flat Poisson floor that the AR spectral model has no term for, and we fit them only where the process rises above that floor (Supplementary Methods).

Because no recording satisfied the AR(1) assumption, the estimators disagreed. Across the AR(1) and AR($p$) fits in the ACF and the PSD, the ratio of the largest reported timescale to the smallest ranged from 1.3 to 7.3 with a median of 4.7; including the two time-domain fits it ranged from 1.3 to 179 with a median of 7.6. The largest disagreements were in the recordings with the strongest rhythms -- motor ECoG, posterior alpha and mouse LFP -- where the AR(1) fits report a fast decay and the AR($p$) fits a slower relaxation once the rhythm is described rather than absorbed. The most extreme single case is a lagged regression on sensorimotor MEG that returned 2.0 s against 11 ms from the ACF and 23 ms from the PSD, reading a sustained beta rhythm as persistence. The smallest disagreements, under 1.5-fold, were in resting-state fMRI and macaque ECoG, the two recordings whose spectra have the least structure to disagree about.

The modes say what each recording contains (\figpanel{fig:realdata}{b,c}). Decomposing each AR($p$) fit, and keeping only the modes whose timescale the analysis band can resolve (Supplementary Methods), leaves between one and five modes per trace. Resting-state fMRI and macaque ECoG are described by one mode. The rest need two or more, and the second is not a detail: in posterior alpha the largest mode is an 89 ms rhythm and the second an 18 ms relaxation, in rat hippocampus a 127 ms rhythm and a 45 ms relaxation, in mouse LFP a 119 ms rhythm and a 39 ms relaxation. In each of these a single reported $\tau$ is not a compromise between the two numbers but a choice of one of them, made by the estimator. Identifying the dominant relaxation mode, and reporting the rhythm beside it, is what a higher-order model provides that a single timescale cannot.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

A neural timescale is a parameter of a model. When the model is the AR(1) process, as it is for an exponential fit to the ACF, a Lorentzian fit to the PSD, or a lagged regression on the signal, $\tau$ is the relaxation time of that process, and reporting it as a property of a region, a state or a subject is reasonable. When the model is wrong, $\tau$ is still well defined, still precise, still reproducible and still comparable across conditions; it is no longer the quantity the interpretation is about. Our simulations show that the two situations look identical from the outside, and our recordings show that neural data are usually in the second one.

Three checks tell them apart at no cost. The first is agreement between independent estimators: when the data are AR(1) the six agree to within 2\%, and when they are not they disagree by up to 46-fold in simulation and 179-fold in the recordings. The second is the fraction of the spectrum, or of the ACF, that the fitted model leaves unexplained. The third is the order BIC selects. If independent estimators agree to within about 1.5-fold, the AR(1) model leaves a few percent unexplained, and BIC selects a low order, a scalar $\tau$ is defensible. None of the eight recordings met that bar.

The four failures call for different responses. Model mismatch calls for a higher order, and the resulting model is readable as a list of decay times and rhythms, of which the dominant relaxation mode is the timescale an AR(1) fit was trying to report. An oscillation that is part of the dynamics is an AR(2) process and is best modeled, which recovers its frequency and its envelope decay; an oscillation added to the signal, such as a stimulus-driven steady-state response, is not autoregressive at any order and must be avoided, which is possible in the PSD and not in the ACF. Finite samples set a floor of about $200\tau$ per estimate, and non-stationarity pushes against that floor: a window short enough to be stationary is often too short to be precise, and the resolution is either to average short windows across trials or to design experiments whose states last long enough to be estimated. A scale-free process has no timescale, and a number reported for it describes the analysis window.

The last point has a measurable consequence in real recordings. Cut into windows of a given length, the spread of estimates across windows was about an order of magnitude larger than finite sampling predicts (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}), so at usable window lengths the dominant source of variability is change in the signal, not estimation noise. A confidence interval computed from the sampling formula understates the uncertainty of a windowed estimate; one computed from the spread across windows does not.

\subsection{Recommendations}

We suggest the following when reporting a neural timescale. \textit{First}, state the model rather than only the estimator, for example ``the relaxation time of an AR(1) fit to the 1-100 Hz spectrum'' rather than ``the neural timescale''. \textit{Second}, report the ratio of recording length to estimated timescale, and treat estimates below roughly $200\tau$ as precision-limited. \textit{Third}, report the fraction of the spectrum or autocorrelation left unexplained by the fitted model. \textit{Fourth}, report at least one independent estimator; disagreement beyond about 1.5-fold is evidence of model mismatch rather than of noise. \textit{Fifth}, if the data contain a strong narrowband rhythm, prefer spectral-domain estimation and state whether the rhythm is being modeled or excluded. \textit{Sixth}, if an AR($p$) model is used, report the modes as $(\tau, f)$ pairs with their importances and the number needed to reproduce the fit, not the coefficient vector. \textit{Finally}, when windowing, take the confidence interval from the spread across windows rather than from the sampling formula.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our simulations treat stationary processes, and non-stationarity is addressed only through windowing; it remains the hardest of the four failures because reducing it by shortening windows increases finite-sample bias. Seven of the eight recordings are continuous field potentials or a haemodynamic signal, and only one is a spike train, for which binning and Poisson variability add a flat floor that the AR spectral model cannot represent \cite{zeraati_flexible_2022, meyer_inferring_2019}; we handle it only by restricting the fit band, and a model with an explicit observation-noise term would treat it properly. The real-data analysis deliberately applies one pipeline to every recording, so the preprocessing a specialist would apply -- artifact rejection, burst detection, oscillation removal -- is absent by design, and the mismatch we report is an upper bound on what careful per-dataset treatment would leave. Finally, the mode decomposition is exact for the fitted model but inherits its assumptions. It is a parsimonious description of a spectrum, and whether a given mode corresponds to a separable biological process is a question the model cannot answer: summing two independent AR(1) processes yields a model whose fitted poles are pulled away from both original timescales, because the sum is not itself autoregressive. The same description does suggest a richer target for the hierarchy literature than one number -- two or three decay times per region and the rhythms attached to them -- and whether those vary along the cortical hierarchy \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014, raut_organization_2020, chaudhuri_large-scale_2015} is a question a single-timescale summary cannot pose.

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:smethods}

\subsubsection*{Conventions}

We write the AR($p$) model as in eq. \ref{eq:arp}, so that the characteristic polynomial is $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$ and stationarity requires all of its roots to lie inside the unit circle. Sign conventions differ between texts and the two common polynomials are reciprocals of one another; we use this one throughout, so that ``pole inside the unit circle'' means ``stationary''. The one-sided spectral density is
\begin{align}
S(f) = \frac{2\sigma^2/f_s}{\left|1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p}\varphi_k e^{-i 2\pi f k/f_s}\right|^{2}}, \qquad 0 \le f \le f_s/2,
\end{align}
normalized so that its integral is the process variance, matching Welch's method \cite{welch_use_1967}. For $p=1$ this is the discrete-time Lorentzian whose knee frequency is $1/(2\pi\tau)$. The theoretical autocovariance is obtained by solving the Yule-Walker system for the first $p+1$ lags and recursing thereafter.

\subsubsection*{Estimators}

Six estimators were compared. They differ in the domain they are fit in and in the model order, and they otherwise share everything: each consumes the same mean-centered signal and the same Welch power spectrum \cite{welch_use_1967}, so that differences between them cannot be attributed to preprocessing.

Making that sharing exact requires care with the autocorrelation. A spectral fit is restricted to an analysis band, 1-100 Hz for most of the electrophysiological recordings here, whereas the sample autocorrelation of the raw signal contains everything in the recording, including drift below the band. Fitting one estimator to the band and the other to the whole signal compares two answers to two different questions. We therefore compute the autocorrelation from the same band-limited spectrum the spectral estimators see, by inverse Fourier transform, with bins excluded as line noise filled by log-linear interpolation from their neighbours rather than zeroed, so that notching does not ring in the lag domain. The difference is not cosmetic: on rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 the raw autocorrelation gives an AR(1) timescale of 938 ms, set by slow drift the spectral fit never saw, against 14 ms once both estimators describe the same band. One further correction is needed. Welch's estimate is the average periodogram of tapered segments, so the autocovariance it implies is the true autocovariance multiplied by the taper's own autocorrelation, which for a Hann window has fallen to 0.66 at a lag one quarter of the segment length. Left in place this taper masquerades as a faster decay, so we divide it out and discard the lags at which the taper retains less than a fifth of its energy, where the correction amplifies noise faster than it removes bias.

In the time domain, the AR(1) estimator is a conditional least-squares regression of $x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$, and the AR($p$) estimator uses Burg's method \cite{burg_maximum_1975}. In the autocorrelation domain, the model is $A\,\rho_p(k) + B$, where $\rho_p$ is the exact AR($p$) autocorrelation, $A$ absorbs a white-noise floor at lag zero and $B$ a constant offset; for $p=1$ this is the exponential-plus-offset model standard in this literature \cite{murray_hierarchy_2014}. In the spectral domain, the model is eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} with a free scale, fit to $\log_{10}$ power, the convention of spectral parameterization methods \cite{donoghue_parameterizing_2020}.

Robust (soft-L1) regression is used in the spectral domain only, and only when the signal contains a rhythm: in simulation, for the added sinusoid and the AR(2) oscillator; in real data, for every recording, since all of them contain a narrowband peak. It is offered in the spectral domain because a rhythm is an outlier there, occupying a handful of bins. In the lag domain a rhythm perturbs every lag, so down-weighting large residuals discards the fit rather than the rhythm, and robustness is not offered. Robustness is not free even in the spectrum: when there is no outlier to reject, down-weighting the tails of an ordinary residual distribution biases the fit, so it is a claim about the data and not a default. It is also a trade-off between the two things a fit can report. On an AR(1) background at $\tau = 50$ ms plus a broadband AR(2) rhythm at 10 Hz, which unlike a sinusoid the model can represent, the robust spectral fit recovered the aperiodic timescale at 53 ms over 20 realizations while discounting the rhythm; without robustness the fit found the rhythm in all 20 realizations at a median of 9.4 Hz and returned the aperiodic timescale at 68 ms. A study that wants both should report them from two fits and say so.

Both the autocorrelation and spectral fits are optimized over reflection coefficients rather than over the AR coefficients directly. Every point in the box $|k_i| < 1$ corresponds to a stationary model, the parameters are all of order one, and the fit cannot wander into a region where mode timescales are undefined; without this reparameterization 48\% of the spectral fits to real data returned non-stationary parameters. An alternative repair, useful when a fit is inherited from elsewhere, is to reflect any pole with $|\lambda| \ge 1$ to $1/\bar\lambda$ and divide the scale by $|\lambda|^2$; on the unit circle $|1 - \lambda z^{-1}|^2 = |\lambda|^2 |1 - z^{-1}/\bar\lambda|^2$, so this leaves the model spectrum unchanged while making the process stationary. Multiple starting points are used because the objective is non-convex when a rhythm is present.

Two further requirements are imposed on the fitted model, because the model is being used as an instrument for measuring a timescale rather than only as a description of the data. First, the model must contain a relaxation mode. Nothing in a least-squares fit requires this: a converged, well-fitting AR($p$) can place every pole in a conjugate pair, in which case it has no decay time to report. Before the constraint, a third of the fits to our real recordings came back with no relaxation mode, having described the data well. We therefore factor one real pole out of the characteristic polynomial, $A(z^{-1}) = (1 - a z^{-1})A_{\mathrm{rest}}(z^{-1})$ with $0 < a < 1$, and parameterize the remainder by reflection coefficients, so that a relaxation mode exists by construction, exactly as stationarity does. The requirement costs one order and is applied at every order, including two; on an AR(2) oscillator BIC then selects order three. Exempting order two was tried and abandoned, because it let a purely rhythmic AR(2) fit report the decay of its envelope as an aperiodic timescale. The constraint costs essentially nothing in fit: across our recordings the median change in each estimator's own objective was $+0.003$ for the lag-domain fit and $-0.000$ for the spectral fit. Second, the reported aperiodic timescale is the most important \emph{non-rhythmic} mode, where non-rhythmic means $f\tau < 1$, and a real pole qualifies trivially. A conjugate pair that rings for a third of a cycle is a damped relaxation, and reporting nothing for a model containing one would be an artifact of the algebra; and the guaranteed real pole is guaranteed to exist, not to matter. On mouse LFP the constrained spectral fit parks its required pole at 0.6 ms with negligible importance while the aperiodic structure sits in an 88 ms pair ringing 0.57 of a cycle. Importance, not pole type, decides.

Spectra are estimated by Welch's method, except for fMRI. A 296-sample record cannot afford Welch's trade: segmenting it leaves either too few frequency bins to fit or too few segments to average. We therefore use a multitaper estimate of the whole record \cite{thomson_spectrum_1982}, with time-bandwidth product $NW = 5$, which gives twice as many bins as the best Welch setting at a quarter of the bin-to-bin variability. Because adjacent multitaper bins inside the resolution bandwidth are not independent, the spectral fits use bins thinned to that spacing; leaving them in makes the record look eight times larger than it is, and order selection then buys orders it cannot support.

Models fit in either domain are scored in both. Spectral fit is the coefficient of determination of $\log_{10}$ power over the analysis band, with the overall scale refit; autocorrelation fit is the coefficient of determination over the fitted lags, with $A$ and $B$ refit. The two objectives are not equivalent, and the asymmetry between them is one of the results of the benchmark below.

\subsubsection*{Model order}

Order is selected by BIC \cite{schwarz_estimating_1978} in the domain the model is fit in: from the conditional Gaussian likelihood in the time domain, and from the residual sum of squares in the autocorrelation and spectral domains. The search runs over a log-spaced grid of orders up to 20. In the lag domain, neighbouring lags are strongly dependent, so the criterion uses an effective sample size, the number of lags divided by the lag at which the autocorrelation first falls below $1/e$, and stops early once it has passed its minimum. In the spectral domain the full grid is searched, because the grid is not uniform and the criterion is not unimodal over it. When the selected order reaches the top of the range this is recorded and reported, because it indicates a process the AR class cannot represent at any practical order rather than a well-determined order.

\subsubsection*{Modes}

The AR($p$) recursion in eq. \ref{eq:arp} has a characteristic polynomial $z^p - \varphi_1 z^{p-1} - \cdots - \varphi_p$, whose roots $\lambda_k$ we call the poles. Stationarity requires $|\lambda_k| < 1$. Dividing by $z^p$ gives
\begin{align}
1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} \varphi_k z^{-k} = \prod_{k=1}^{p} (1 - \lambda_k z^{-1}), \label{eq:factor}
\end{align}
so the spectral denominator in eq. \ref{eq:arp_psd} factorizes into first-order terms and each pole contributes a decay time and, when complex, a frequency (eq. \ref{eq:mode}). Because the coefficients are real, complex poles come in conjugate pairs, and we combine each pair into a single damped oscillatory mode equivalent to a real AR(2) factor. A real pole is a pure relaxation mode. The term is borrowed from linear systems theory, where the modes of a linear system are the eigenvalues that govern how it relaxes.

Two points about this factorization are easy to state wrongly. It is a factorization of the transfer function, not a decomposition of the signal into independent AR(1) processes: the factors multiply in the spectrum, and an AR($p$) process is a cascade of first-order filters driven by one input. Conversely, the same poles do appear additively in the time domain. The impulse response is $\psi_n = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^n$, so the response to an input is a sum of decaying modes driven by a shared innovation, and the autocovariance decomposes the same way,
\begin{align}
\gamma(k) = \sum_j A_j \lambda_j^{\,k}. \label{eq:acfmodes}
\end{align}
Grouping the conjugate terms, the autocorrelation of any AR($p$) process is a sum of exponential decays and damped cosines, one per mode. The product form and the sum form are the factored and partial-fraction representations of the same rational function, and they are not transforms of each other: a product in the frequency domain is a convolution in the lag domain, not a sum, so a mode's additive term in eq. \ref{eq:acfmodes} is not the inverse transform of its multiplicative factor in the spectrum. What the partial-fraction form costs is separability: each residue $A_j$ depends on every pole, so a mode's contribution to the autocorrelation is not a property of that mode alone. This is also why a lag-domain AR($p$) fit to an added sinusoid is biased (main text, \figpanel{fig:osc}{c}): the residues are not free parameters, so the model has no parameter for the mixing weight of an external mixture. Because the modes share an input, the process variance does not decompose into per-mode shares.

\subsubsection*{Mode importance}

Writing $g_k(f)$ for the log of the $k$th pole factor, the model log spectrum is $\log \sigma^2 - \sum_k g_k(f)$: modes are additive in log power. Deleting mode $k$ and refitting the overall scale by least squares therefore leaves a residual whose root-mean-square in $\log_{10}$ power is exactly $\mathrm{sd}_f\{g_k\}/\ln 10$, and we use this as the mode's importance. An Itakura-Saito variant, equal to the asymptotic Whittle log-likelihood degradation \cite{whittle_estimation_1953, itakura_analysis_1968}, gives the same ranking in every case we examined. The error of a model retaining an arbitrary subset of modes is a quadratic form in the retained set, so the best subset of each size can be evaluated in closed form; the dominant modes are the smallest subset reproducing the fitted log spectrum with $R^2 \ge 0.99$.

For the real recordings, a mode is reported only if the analysis band can resolve its timescale. A mode slower than $1/(2\pi f_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $f_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the lower edge of the fitted band, has its knee below the band, so its timescale is an extrapolation of the model past the data rather than a measurement; left in, these are the near-unit-root poles that report a 393 s timescale from a 300 s recording, and they carry high importance precisely because deleting them changes the lowest frequencies. The criterion discards 8\% of the fitted modes. Likewise a rhythmic mode counts as detected only if its frequency lies inside the fitted range.

\subsubsection*{Finite-sample theory}

Computing confidence intervals around an estimated $\varphi$ has a long history \cite{mann_statistical_1943}. For a mean-corrected least-squares AR(1) fit on $n$ samples, the classical results are $\mathbb{E}[\hat\varphi] - \varphi \approx -(1+3\varphi)/n$ \cite{marriott_bias_1954, kendall_note_1954} and
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[ (\hat{\varphi} - \varphi)^2 ] &\approx \frac{1 - \varphi^2}{n-1}. \label{eq:var}
\end{align}
Propagating both through eq. \ref{eq:tau} to second order, and writing $T = n/f_s$ for the recording duration, gives in the regime $\tau f_s \gg 1$ that all neural recordings occupy
\begin{align}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[\hat\tau] - \tau}{\tau} \approx -\frac{2\tau}{T}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{sd}[\hat\tau]}{\tau} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{T}}. \label{eq:finite}
\end{align}
Both depend on the recording only through $T/\tau$, and both were verified against Monte Carlo. Confidence intervals are obtained by mapping the interval for $\hat\varphi$ through eq. \ref{eq:tau}, which preserves coverage because the map is monotone. Eq. \ref{eq:var} is increasingly biased as $\varphi \to 1$; intervals may instead be computed as a weighted average of the least-squares and Cauchy estimators \cite{gallagher_small_2008}, which is the construction used in \figpanel{fig:finite}{a,b}.

\subsubsection*{The integrated timescale}

As a model-free reference we use $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \int_0^\infty \rho(t)\,dt$ \cite{sokal_monte_1997, priestley_spectral_1981}, evaluated with the trapezoid convention so that a sampled exponential returns its continuous-time $\tau$ rather than $\tau + 1/(2f_s)$. Equivalently $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = S(0)/(4\gamma(0))$. It equals $\tau$ for an AR(1) process, is the variance-weighted mean of component timescales for a sum of independent AR(1) processes, and diverges for a long-memory process. It is not the envelope decay time of an oscillator: for a damped oscillator the anticorrelated troughs of the autocorrelation cancel most of the integral, which is why the AR(2) oscillator of the benchmark has $\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = 9$ ms and an envelope decay of 50 ms. For an oscillatory process, how long activity stays correlated and how fast a perturbation decays are different questions with different answers.

\subsubsection*{Simulated processes}

Five processes were used in the benchmark, each with a closed-form spectrum and autocorrelation, simulated at 500 Hz for 100 s with 200 repetitions. Each isolates one way the AR(1) assumption can fail. \textit{AR(1)} with $\tau=50$ ms is the positive control: the model is correct, so an estimator that fails here fails for reasons of its own. \textit{AR(1) plus sinusoid} adds $A\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi)$ with random phase, parameterized by the ratio of oscillatory to aperiodic variance; the sinusoid is not an autoregressive process at any order, and it stands for the rhythm-contaminated recording. \textit{AR(2) oscillator} has poles at $|\lambda| e^{\pm i\theta}$ with $|\lambda| = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}$ and $\theta = 2\pi f_0/f_s$; it is a rhythm the model represents exactly, and its role is to separate a rhythm that must be avoided from one that should be modeled. \textit{AR($p$)} is a constructed AR(8) with five modes, a 100 ms and a 2 ms relaxation and damped rhythms at 10, 40 and 180 Hz. It is the only process inside the model family but above first order, which is what makes it necessary: every other failing process confounds model mismatch with order mismatch, and this one isolates the second. Its rhythms all ring for under a cycle, as the real recordings do, so that the rule an estimator is asked to apply -- the most important non-rhythmic mode -- has an unambiguous target. The AR(7) read mode by mode in \figpanel{fig:modes}{} is a different construction chosen for a different job: two relaxations of comparable importance an order of magnitude apart, so that no single timescale describes it, and rhythms that ring for more than a cycle, so that an envelope decay cannot be mistaken for a period. \textit{Power law} is a long-memory ARFIMA process \cite{granger_introduction_1980, hosking_fractional_1981} simulated exactly by circulant embedding \cite{davies_tests_1987}; it is outside the family and has no characteristic timescale, so there is nothing for an estimator to recover.

Outside AR(1) the target is not given and has to be stated. We take it to be whatever the constructed process gives under the same rule the estimator is asked to apply, computed from the true poles: the relaxation time for AR(1), the envelope decay for the AR(2) oscillator, and for the AR(8) the most important non-rhythmic mode. Defining the AR(8) target as the slowest relaxation by construction would score estimators against a quantity they were never asked for, since the 10 Hz mode of that model rings only half a cycle and is both non-rhythmic by that rule and more important than the 100 ms relaxation. Every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation was verified against long simulations.

\subsubsection*{Benchmark results}

When the process is AR(1), all six estimators recover the 50 ms timescale to within 2\% (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_bench}{c}, first column). Adding a 10 Hz sinusoid carrying as much variance as the aperiodic component spreads the estimates over a factor of nine: the autocorrelation fit returns 15 ms and the direct time-domain fit 84 ms, while both spectral fits land within 0.5\% of the truth. For the AR(2) oscillator the three AR(1) estimators return 12, 127 and 229 ms for a 50 ms envelope decay, while all three AR($p$) fits recover it to within 3\% and the rhythm to within 0.05 Hz. For the constructed AR(8) the three AR($p$) estimators return 50 to 54 ms against a target of 50 ms, while the three AR(1) estimators spread over a factor of 401, from 78 ms for the autocorrelation fit to 1.07 s for the lagged regression, and the AR(1) spectral fit runs to the 20 s bound of its search. The long-memory process produces estimates from 4 to 86 ms, a twenty-fold range, every one of them stable across repetitions.

Model mismatch tracks these failures. An AR(1) spectral fit leaves 0.8\% of the log spectrum unexplained for the AR(1) process, but 2.5\% with an added sinusoid, 24\% for the AR(2) oscillator, 55\% for the AR(8) and 26\% for the power law; AR($p$) reduces these to between 0.1\% and 4.0\%. BIC returns $p=1$ for AR(1) and for the added sinusoid, $p=8$ for the AR(8), and runs to the top of the search range for the power law. Fitting each process's exact autocorrelation, which is what any consistent estimator converges to with infinite data, the mismatch collapses to zero at the correct order for the three autoregressive processes and plateaus for the added sinusoid, which no finite order removes.

The domain a model is fit in changes the model. Across the four processes that are not AR(1), fits to the spectrum describe the autocorrelation nearly as well as fits to the autocorrelation do, leaving between 0.4\% and 7\% of it unexplained, with the added sinusoid the sole exception at 90\%. Fits to the autocorrelation describe the spectrum badly, leaving 12\% unexplained for the AR(2) oscillator, 7\% for the AR(8), and for the added sinusoid and the long-memory process many times the total spectral variance; on one realization of the long-memory process, the AR(5) model chosen in the lag domain has a spectrum spanning eleven orders of magnitude across the analysis band, where the data span two. Both domains use the same model, reparameterization, order selection and band, and the autocorrelation fit is the better of the two at describing the autocorrelation, so this is not an optimization artifact. A model estimated in the frequency domain is a usable description of the signal in either domain; a model estimated in the lag domain is not. On a scale-free process the lag-domain fit also returns an estimate proportional to the recording length, $\hat\tau \propto T$ over three decades, where the same estimator on an AR(1) process is flat.

\subsubsection*{Datasets and processing}

Eight publicly available recordings were used, selected to span species, recording modality and oscillatory regime rather than to address any single biological question. \textit{fMRI}: ABIDE resting-state BOLD \cite{di_martino_autism_2014}, six participants from the UM site, preprocessed by C-PAC without temporal filtering, TR 2 s. We take the precuneus, left and right, giving twelve traces. A single 296-sample BOLD run gives a spectrum too noisy to fit, and averaging over regions is the obvious remedy but the wrong one: regions differ in exactly the property being measured, and precuneus, medial prefrontal and occipital spectra have visibly different shapes in these data, so an average over all 111 Harvard-Oxford regions would describe no region. One default-mode hub keeps the average interpretable and makes a trace here the same kind of object as a channel elsewhere. The unfiltered pipeline is used deliberately, since the usual 0.01-0.1 Hz band-pass removes most of the spectrum a timescale is estimated from. \textit{MEG}: the FieldTrip cortico-muscular coherence recording \cite{schoffelen_neuronal_2005, oostenveld_fieldtrip_2011}, eight sensorimotor magnetometers during isometric contraction. \textit{EEG}: MPI-LEMON eyes-closed rest \cite{babayan_mind_2019}, posterior channels. \textit{ECoG}: pre-DBS primary motor cortex in Parkinson disease \cite{cole_nonsinusoidal_2017}; and macaque visual cortex during a grating task \cite{nagasaka_multidimensional_2011}. \textit{LFP}: mouse Neuropixels in VISam during natural movie viewing \cite{siegle_survey_2021}; and rat hippocampal CA1/CA3 \cite{mizuseki_theta_2009, mizuseki_hc3_2013}. \textit{Spiking}: spike counts of eight VISp units from the same Neuropixels session \cite{siegle_survey_2021}, binned at 50 ms over the same 300 s of natural movie. The neural-timescale literature rests largely on spike-count autocorrelations, so this is the one modality whose absence would be conspicuous.

Electrophysiological recordings were resampled to 250 Hz and fit over 1-100 Hz, with line-noise fundamentals and harmonics excluded from the spectral fits. fMRI carries its own band, 0.01-0.24 Hz, because it is sampled three orders of magnitude more slowly. The spike counts are fit over 0.05-2 Hz rather than the 0.05-8 Hz their sampling rate allows: a spike train contributes a flat Poisson floor on top of whatever the membrane dynamics contribute, the AR spectral model $\sigma^2/|A|^2$ has no additive term for a floor, and above about 1.5 Hz the binned-count spectrum is within a factor of 1.5 of its own floor. Fitted over the full band the AR(1) model was fitting mostly shot noise, leaving 95\% of the log spectrum unexplained; restricting the fit to where the process is still above its shot noise improves it tenfold and brings the two spectral estimates from disagreeing by a factor of eleven to a factor of two. The criterion is that the process remain above its own shot noise, not a fixed frequency, and the band over which a spike-count model is fitted is a modelling choice that changes the answer and should be reported. No other filtering, artifact rejection or oscillation removal was applied.

\subsubsection*{Windowing real recordings}

Each recording was cut into non-overlapping windows of increasing length, from 0.5 s upward, and every window analysed with the identical pipeline (Supplementary \figpanel{fig:s_windows}{}). Window length is measured in units of the timescale being estimated rather than in seconds, since a 1 s window is generous for a 4 ms timescale and hopeless for a 1 s one. Measured this way, the median estimate is close to window-independent: in six of the eight recordings it stayed between 0.73 and 1.9 times the estimate from the longest window, over more than three orders of magnitude of window length, the exceptions being macaque visual ECoG and the mouse spike counts, which sit at about a quarter of their final value until the window exceeds a thousand times the timescale. This is not what the same analysis showed before the autocorrelation was corrected. Taking the autocorrelation from the raw signal instead of from the analysis band, estimates collapse by up to two orders of magnitude at the shortest windows and climb steadily with window length, which reads as a window-length effect on the brain's timescales and is instead drift below the fit band entering the lag domain. An artifact of this shape is easy to produce by accident and hard to notice.

The spread between windows is a different matter. If finite sampling were the only source of variability, the spread of estimates across windows of a given length would follow the AR(1) sampling bound of eq. \ref{eq:finite}. At the longest windows available, the spectral estimate exceeded that bound in all eight recordings, by a median factor of 6 and by up to 30, and the autocorrelation estimate in all eight, by a median factor of 7 and by up to 29. This does not go away when the autocorrelation is corrected. At the durations where an estimate becomes precise enough to report, the dominant source of variability is change in the signal over time, and a timescale reported for a long recording is an average over a quantity that was changing throughout it.

\subsubsection*{Code}

All analyses use an open-source Python module with 200 unit tests covering, among other things, exact reconstruction of the AR($p$) spectrum and autocorrelation from the mode decomposition, agreement between the closed-form mode importance and a brute-force deletion computation, agreement of every theoretical spectrum and autocorrelation with long simulations, the guaranteed relaxation mode, and agreement of eq. \ref{eq:finite} with Monte Carlo. Code, figure scripts and result tables are available at \href{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}{https://github.com/voytekresearch/timescale-methods}.
